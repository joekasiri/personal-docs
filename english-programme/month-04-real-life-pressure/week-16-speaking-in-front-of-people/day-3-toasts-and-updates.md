## Learning objectives

- Give a 1–2 minute toast with a clear structure and a strong final line.
- Give a short spoken update to a group: the news, the details, what happens next.
- Speak slowly and warmly with pauses, so a room can follow.
- Keep a toast kind, inclusive and short.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's feature: toast rhythm.** Toasts are slower than conversation. Speakers **pause after each idea**, **smile** while speaking (you can hear it), **stress the person's name** and slow right down for the **final line**, then raise the volume a little on "Cheers!" or "To Ana!"

Practice sentences (stand up, glass or cup in hand, / = pause):

1. "Can I have everyone's attention for a sec? / Thank you."
2. "I'll keep this short, / I promise."
3. "For those who don't know me, / I'm Alex, / and I've worked with Ana for three years."
4. "One thing everyone knows about ANA / is that she's always on time. / Except today."
5. "I'll never forget the time / she drove four hours / just to bring me soup."
6. "We're gonna miss you. / A lot."
7. "So please, / raise your glasses / to ANA."
8. "To Ana! / CHEERS!"
9. "Quick update on the trip, / everyone. / The big news is: / we got the cabin."
10. "That's all from me. / Thanks, guys."

**Clip task:** search "short birthday toast speech", "farewell speech coworker short", "wedding toast best friend short and sweet" (choose friendly, clean ones). Speak along with one full toast. Copy the pauses and the slow, warm final line. Method: [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (30 min): Toasts and updates

### Explanation

**Toasts.** A toast is a short speech to honour someone, usually with a drink in hand (any drink: juice and water are fine). In the US you might give one at a birthday dinner, a farewell, a wedding, a team celebration, or a holiday meal. The best toasts are **short (1–2 minutes), personal, kind and clear**. Nobody wants a ten-minute toast.

**The toast template:**

| Part | What you say | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Get attention | ask for attention, say who you are if needed | "Can I have everyone's attention for a sec? For those who don't know me, I'm Alex, Ana's teammate." |
| 2. Connection | how you know them | "I've worked with Ana for three years." |
| 3. One quality + one short story | what's special about them, with a real example | "Ana is the most generous person I know. When I got sick in my first month, she drove over with soup. I'd known her for two weeks." |
| 4. The occasion | why we're here | "And now she's off to Toronto for an amazing new job." |
| 5. A wish + raise your glass | a warm final line | "Ana, we're gonna miss you so much. So please, raise your glasses. To Ana!" |

**Toast rules:** keep stories kind (no embarrassing secrets, no ex-partners, nothing that only two people understand); everyone in the room should understand your jokes; speak to the room *and* to the person (look at them at the end); don't read from your phone with your head down (a few notes are okay).

**Updates.** A short group update shares news with people who care: friends planning a trip, a club, a family, a team at lunch. It needs a simple structure so people can follow without slides:

| Part | Example |
|---|---|
| 1. Signal | "Quick update on the trip, everyone." |
| 2. The headline | "The big news is: we got the cabin!" |
| 3. Two or three details | "It's June 12th to 15th, it sleeps eight, and it's $60 each." |
| 4. What's next / what I need | "I need everyone's money by Friday." |
| 5. Close | "That's all from me. Any questions?" |

Both toasts and updates are perfect for "talking without thinking": the structure is fixed, so your brain only needs to fill in the content.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Can I have everyone's attention for a sec? | you start a toast or update | "Can I have everyone's attention for a sec? Thanks." |
| I'll keep this short. | you promise a short speech (people relax) | "I'll keep this short, I know the food's coming." |
| For those who don't know me, I'm… | some people don't know you | "For those who don't know me, I'm Alex, Sara's old roommate." |
| I've known [name] for… | your connection | "I've known Sara for ten years." |
| One thing everyone knows about [name] is… | you introduce their quality | "One thing everyone knows about Leo is that he loves a plan." |
| I'll never forget the time… | a short story | "I'll never forget the time he got us lost in Rome." |
| We're gonna miss you. | farewell | "We're gonna miss you, but we're so proud of you." |
| So please raise your glasses to… | the moment to drink | "So please raise your glasses to Sara and Tom." |
| To [name]! / Cheers! / Here's to [name]! | the final line | "To Sara and Tom! Cheers!" |
| Quick update on [X], everyone. | you start an update | "Quick update on Saturday, everyone." |
| The big news is… | the headline | "The big news is: the venue said yes." |
| Here's where we're at: | you give the current situation | "Here's where we're at: we have six people and two cars." |
| That's all from me. | you finish an update | "That's all from me. Any questions?" |

### Examples

**Model toast 1: a friend's birthday (about 1 minute).**

> "Okay, can I have everyone's attention for a sec? Thank you. I'll keep this short, I know the cake is waiting. / For those who don't know me, I'm Alex, I've known Jess since our first week in this city. / One thing everyone knows about Jess is that she always says yes. Yes to hiking at 5 a.m., yes to karaoke, yes to helping you move a sofa up four floors. I'll never forget when I moved here and didn't know anyone. Jess invited me to her birthday on my *second day*. That was three years ago, and here we are. / Jess, thank you for always saying yes to us. So please, raise your glasses. / To Jess! Happy birthday!"

**Model toast 2: a colleague's farewell (about 1.5 minutes).**

> "Hi everyone, can I just say a few words? I'll keep it short. / For those who don't know me, I'm Alex from the design team, and I've worked with Ana for three years. / Ana is the person you go to when something is on fire. Not literally. Well, once literally — the toaster, 2023. / But seriously, when I joined, I was so nervous I couldn't even present my work. Ana sat with me for an hour before my first review, and said, 'Just tell them the story.' I still do that, every time. / Now she's off to Toronto to lead her own team, and they are so lucky. Ana, we're gonna miss you a lot, and we're so proud of you. So please, raise your glasses. / To Ana!"

**Model toast 3: a friend's engagement (about 1 minute).**

> "Hi everyone! I'm Alex, Tom's friend from college. I'll keep this short. / I've known Tom for twelve years, and for eleven of them, he was hopeless at planning anything. Then he met Mia. / Suddenly Tom had a calendar. Tom had *reservations*. Tom knew what day it was. / Mia, thank you for that. And thank you for making our friend so happy. You two are amazing together. So, here's to Mia and Tom! / Cheers!"

**Model update: a group trip (about 1 minute).**

> "Okay, quick update on the trip, everyone. / The big news is: we got the cabin! / So here's where we're at. It's June 12th to the 15th, Thursday to Sunday. It sleeps eight, so we have room for two more people. And it's sixty dollars each, per night. / What I need from you: send me the money by Friday, and tell me if you're driving or need a ride. / That's all from me. Any questions?"

### Common mistakes

❌ "Let's drink for Ana." / "We drink to the health of Ana."
✅ "To Ana!" / "Here's to Ana!" / "Raise your glasses to Ana."
*Reason: these are the fixed toast phrases in English.*

❌ An inside joke only you and the person understand: "Remember Bob's thing? Ha!"
✅ A short story everyone can follow.
*Reason: a toast is for the whole room.*

❌ An embarrassing story: "…and that's when she threw up on my shoes."
✅ A story that shows something good about them.
*Reason: toasts honour people. Keep it kind, especially in front of family or colleagues.*

❌ A five-minute toast.
✅ One to two minutes.
*Reason: people are holding glasses and food is waiting.*

❌ Reading the whole toast from your phone, head down.
✅ Remember the structure; a few keywords on a card are fine. Look up.
*Reason: eye contact makes it warm.*

❌ An update with no headline: "So, umm, I called some places, and one of them…"
✅ "The big news is: we got the cabin."
*Reason: give the news first, then the details.*

## 3. Speaking drill (60 min)

### Part A: Toast drill (30 min)

1. **Write** (5 min): fill the template for your **real person** (see Before you start). Write only **keywords** for each of the five parts, not full sentences.
2. **3 minutes** (record, standing, glass in hand): give the toast with everything you want to say.
3. **2 minutes** (record): cut to the best story. Pause after each part.
4. **1 minute** (record): five parts, one sentence each, slow warm final line.
5. **Unprepared toasts** (10 min): use three of the [toast cards](exercises.md#day-3-exercises) you haven't done. No writing. 60 seconds each, recorded.

This is a toast version of the [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

### Part B: Live conversation #1 (30 min): speak to a group

**Mission:** speak to a group of 3+ people for 1–2 minutes at least once, and take part in the conversation around it.

Choose one:
- **A toast** at a dinner or drinks (a birthday, someone's news, or just "to good friends").
- **An update** to a group: your week, a trip plan, a project at a club or meetup, news for your family on a video call.
- **A short intro + story** at a meetup when it's your turn.

Try to:
1. Get attention clearly ("Can I have everyone's attention for a sec?" or "Quick update, everyone").
2. Use the structure (toast or update).
3. Project your voice and look at at least three people.
4. Pause at least twice.
5. Finish with a clear final line ("To Jess!", "That's all from me").

**Backup plan:** voice mode with Claude. Paste:

```
You're a group of 5 friends at a dinner: talk among yourselves for a minute (casual, overlapping),
then one of you says "Alex, didn't you want to say something?". I'll give a toast or a short update.
React like a real group (laughs, "aww", someone cheering at the end). Then ask me 2 follow-up questions
about what I said. Do this 3 times with different occasions: a birthday, a farewell, and a group trip update.
No corrections until I say "feedback". Then tell me: was my structure clear, did I pause,
was my final line strong, and up to 5 language mistakes.
```

Log it in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md): who was there, what you said, how it felt (1–10 nerves).
