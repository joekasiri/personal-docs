## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's feature: stretched words and extreme stress.** Exaggeration needs a big voice. The key word is **stretched** and **stressed**, often with a tiny pause before it. "It took, like… a MIIIllion years." "Worst. Movie. EVER." (each word separate, falling).

## 2. Main skill (30 min): Exaggeration

### Explanation

Exaggeration (the technical word is *hyperbole*) means saying something is much bigger, smaller, longer or worse than it really is, **and everyone knows it's not true**. Nobody thinks you actually waited a million years. It is a fast, easy way to be funny and to show your feelings. American English uses it a lot in casual talk. If you only say "It was quite long", you sound polite but a bit flat. "It took, like, a million years" sounds alive.

**Four patterns:**

| Pattern | How it works | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| Impossible numbers | a huge, round number + "like" | "like a million years", "a thousand times", "five hundred emails" |
| Extremes | the most extreme word possible | "I'm starving / dying / exhausted", "freezing", "the worst ever" |
| Crazy comparisons | "so [adj] that…" or "the size of…" | "so cold my coffee froze", "a burger the size of my head" |
| Never / always / forever / everyone | absolute words | "It took forever", "Everyone was there", "I never win anything" |

**"Like" is your friend.** Putting "like" before the number ("like a million") tells people *this is not a real number*. Without it, "It took one million years" sounds strange.

**"Literally"** is used by many Americans to mean "really, very much": "I'm literally starving." Some people think this is wrong, but it is very common in casual speech. Use it in relaxed situations only.

**When not to exaggerate:**
- At work when the numbers matter: "We got, like, a million complaints" in a report can cause panic. Give the real number.
- About serious things: illness, accidents, other people's problems.
- When someone needs exact information (directions, times, prices).

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| It took like a million years. | something was very slow | "The pasta took like a million years to cook." |
| I've told you a thousand times. | you've said it many times (joking) | "I've told you a thousand times, I hate olives!" |
| I'm literally starving. | you're very hungry | "Can we eat? I'm literally starving." |
| I almost died. | something was very funny or scary (light) | "When he fell in the pool, I almost died laughing." |
| It was the size of a [car / my head]. | something was very big | "The burrito was the size of my arm." |
| There were like five hundred people. | it was very crowded | "There were like five hundred people at the café." |
| Worst. [Thing]. Ever. | something was very bad (said word by word) | "Worst. Flight. Ever." |
| It lasted forever. | something was very long | "That meeting lasted forever." |
| So [adjective] that… | a funny result | "It was so hot that my phone stopped working." |
| Everybody and their mom was there. | very crowded (casual, friendly) | "The beach? Everybody and their mom was there." |
| I haven't [done X] in, like, years. | a long time (maybe a week) | "I haven't had a day off in, like, years." |
| I could eat a horse. | very hungry (idiom) | "Dinner? Yes. I could eat a horse." |

### Examples

**Three friends after a concert.**

> **Jess:** Okay, that line for the bathroom.
> **You:** Oh my god. It took, like, a million years. I think I got older in that line.
> **Omar:** There were like five hundred people in front of me.
> **You:** And the drinks were, like, twenty dollars each. For a tiny cup. The size of my thumb.
> **Jess:** Worth it, though.
> **You:** Totally worth it. Best. Show. Ever.

**Coworkers at the coffee machine.**

> **Marcus:** How was the all-hands?
> **You:** It lasted forever. I've seen shorter movies. And the slides — I think there were three hundred.
> **Marcus:** Ha. Anything important?
> **You:** Honestly? One thing. We're moving offices in May. That's the real news. Everything else was just… slides.

*(Notice: you exaggerate the boring parts, but give the real news exactly.)*

**Talking about your apartment (self-deprecating, very safe).**

> **Lin:** So how's the new place?
> **You:** I love it, but it's tiny. Like, *tiny*. I can cook dinner from my bed.
> **Lin:** Ha! That's efficient.
> **You:** And the shower's so small I have to step out to turn around.

### Common mistakes

❌ "It took one million years."
✅ "It took, like, a million years."
*Reason: "like" + a round number shows it's an exaggeration. "One million" sounds like a real count.*

❌ "It was very, very, very big."
✅ "It was huge." / "It was the size of a house."
*Reason: repeating "very" is weak. A strong word or a funny comparison is better.*

❌ At work: "Like a thousand users complained."
✅ "About forty users complained. Which, for us, is a lot."
*Reason: exaggerated numbers at work can be taken as facts.*

❌ "I'm literally dying" when a friend tells you someone is in hospital.
✅ Keep exaggeration for light topics.
*Reason: jokes about death or illness in a serious moment sound careless.*

❌ "The line was long like a million kilometres."
✅ "The line was, like, a mile long." / "The line went around the block."
*Reason: English uses "as long as" or set patterns ("a mile long", "around the block"), not "long like".*

## 3. Speaking drill (60 min): voice mode, exaggeration battle

Tonight you play a game with Claude: a friendly **complaining contest**. You each complain about small, everyday things, and each complaint must be a bit more exaggerated than the last. Then you switch to a normal chat and use exaggeration naturally. See [voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md).

**Step 1 (2 min).** Paste or read:

```
Let's play a game called "Exaggeration Battle". You're my friend RILEY: funny, dramatic, kind.
We take turns complaining about small everyday things (commutes, weather, cooking fails,
technology, tiny apartments, long queues). Each complaint must exaggerate more than the last one,
using big numbers ("like a million"), extremes ("I'm literally dying"), crazy comparisons
("so cold my coffee froze") and "forever / never / everyone". Keep it friendly and silly:
no jokes about people's bodies, illness, or anything serious.
React to my exaggerations like a real friend ("No way, that's nothing — listen to this...").
After 15 minutes, I'll say "switch" and we'll just chat about our week, normal conversation,
and I'll try to use exaggeration naturally. Don't correct me until I say "feedback".
Then tell me: my best 3 exaggerations, any that sounded unnatural (with better versions),
and up to 5 grammar mistakes.
You start: complain about your commute.
```

**Step 2 (15 min).** The battle. Targets: use all four patterns, use "like" before every big number, use at least three sarcastic lines from [Day 1](day-1-sarcasm-and-tone.md) as well.

**Step 3 (15 min).** Say "switch". Chat about your real week. Use exaggeration at least five times, but **give one real, exact fact** after each one ("It took forever. Well, forty minutes.").

**Step 4 (5 min).** Say "feedback". Write down the corrections and the best lines.

**Step 5 (20 min).** Round two. Say: *"Let's do another battle. Topic: the worst vacations or trips we've ever had. Then switch to chatting about weekend plans."* Try to beat your own best exaggeration from round one. Ask for feedback at the end.

**Step 6 (3 min).** Say your three best exaggerations of the night out loud three times each.
