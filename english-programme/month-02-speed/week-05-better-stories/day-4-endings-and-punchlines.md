## 1. Shadowing (20 min)

**Tonight's feature: punchline delivery.** The last line of a funny or surprising story is the **punchline**. Natural speakers do three things: a short **pause** before it, a **short** sentence, and a voice that **drops** at the end (falling intonation), like closing a door. Then they **stop talking**. In the sentences, `||` = pause, `↘` = voice falls.

**Step 1: pause-and-repeat warm-up (5 min).** Say each sentence 3 times. Make the last part short and falling.

1. And the worst part? || It was MY birthday. ↘
2. So YEAH. || I don't go to that gym anymore. ↘
3. Needless to SAY, || I didn't get the job. ↘
4. Long story SHORT, || we're married now. ↘
5. And to this DAY, || he thinks it was HIS idea. ↘
6. Lesson LEARNED. ↘
7. And THAT'S why I never eat sushi at airports. ↘
8. Turns out || it was the wrong HOUSE. ↘
9. Moral of the STORY: || always check the DATE. ↘
10. And we never spoke of it aGAIN. ↘

**Step 2: speak-along (15 min).** Search YouTube for "funny storytime", "stand up storytelling clean" or "comedian tells story podcast". Choose a clip with a clear ending that gets a laugh.

1. Find the last 30 seconds of a story, including the punchline.
2. Listen twice. Notice: how long is the pause? How short is the line? Does the speaker add anything after it?
3. Speak along 5 times. Copy the pause and the falling voice.
4. Last 3 minutes: speak along with only the punchline, 10 times, then say it once alone and record it.

More on the method: [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (40 min): Endings and punchlines

### Explanation

Many learners have strong stories with weak endings. The story just… fades. They say "and… yeah… that's it… umm", and the listener isn't sure if it's over. That moment feels awkward for everyone, and it's often where people freeze.

A good ending does one job: it tells the listener **"the story is over, now react"**. There are three simple types.

1. **The result.** What happened in the end. "Long story short, we missed the flight." "Needless to say, I didn't get the job."
2. **The punchline.** A short, surprising or funny final line. It often reveals one last fact. "And the worst part? It was my own birthday party." A punchline needs a pause before it and silence after it.
3. **The lesson or the "now".** How the story changed you, or how things are today. "Lesson learned: always check the date." "To this day, I can't look at a pineapple." "So now I always carry a charger."

You can combine them: result + lesson. "Long story short, the bug was a typo. So now I double-check everything. Like, everything."

Two rules:

- **Don't explain the punchline.** If the listener doesn't laugh, don't say "because, you know, it was funny because…". Just move on: "Anyway…" (see [Week 3: changing the topic](../../month-01-foundations/week-03-small-talk/day-5-changing-topic-and-ending.md)).
- **Stop after the ending.** Don't add new details. "So yeah." with a falling voice is a perfectly good final full stop. Then let the other person react. If you know they'll want to talk, hand over with a question, which you'll practise in Week 8.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So yeah. ↘ | the simplest, most common "the end" signal | "…and I had to walk home in the rain. So yeah." |
| Long story short… | you skip the boring middle and give the result | "Long story short, I got the apartment." |
| Needless to say… | the result is obvious (often bad) | "Needless to say, we didn't go back." |
| And the worst part? / And the best part? | you save one final surprising fact for the end | "And the best part? He paid for everything." |
| And that's how I ended up… | you explain how you got to a surprising situation | "And that's how I ended up living in Denver." |
| And that's why I never… | your ending is a rule you now follow | "And that's why I never reply-all." |
| Lesson learned. | short, often funny, after a bad experience | "I forgot to save the file. Lesson learned." |
| Moral of the story: … | a joke lesson at the end | "Moral of the story: don't trust a 'quick question'." |
| To this day… | the effect still continues now | "To this day, my sister calls me 'Captain Ketchup'." |
| So now (every time)… | how you behave now because of the story | "So now every time I fly, I pack two chargers." |
| I still can't believe… | you're still surprised | "I still can't believe she said yes." |
| Anyway, that's my (crazy airport / worst date) story. | you close the story and give the floor back | "Anyway, that's my worst interview story." |

### Examples

**Example 1: weak vs strong ending**

Weak:
> "…and then the manager came, and he looked at the bill, and he said it was a mistake, and… yeah… so we paid less… and… that's it, I think."

Strong:
> "…and then the manager comes over, looks at the bill, and goes, 'Oh, you're the guy from the review.' || Long story short, dinner was free. And to this day, I have no idea what review he was talking about."

**Example 2: a punchline, then the listener reacts**

> A: …so I spend the whole morning preparing for this huge presentation, right? Slides, notes, everything. I walk into the room, and it's empty. || Turns out it was moved to Zoom. An hour ago.
> B: No way! What did you do?
> A: Ran back to my desk, joined twenty minutes late. Lesson learned: always check your calendar.

**Example 3: result + "so now"**

> "Long story short, the airline lost my bag for five days. So now I never check a bag. Ever. Even for two weeks, it's carry-on only."

**Example 4: closing the turn**

> "…and he still owes me forty bucks. Anyway, that's my worst roommate story. What about you, have you ever had a nightmare roommate?"

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| ❌ …and yeah, that's it, that's the story, so, umm… | ✅ …needless to say, we didn't go back. So yeah. ↘ | Finish with one clear line and a falling voice, then stop. |
| ❌ Needless to say, but we didn't go back. | ✅ Needless to say, we didn't go back. | No "but" after "needless to say". |
| ❌ Long story short, so, I was at the airport, and then… [new details] | ✅ Long story short, I missed the flight. | "Long story short" must be followed by the result, not more story. |
| ❌ And the worst part is that it was my birthday. | ✅ And the worst part? || It was my birthday. | The question + pause version is much stronger. |
| ❌ (after the punchline) …because, you know, it was funny because she was the boss. | ✅ (after the punchline) Silence, or "Anyway…" | Never explain your own punchline. |
| ❌ Until this day, I don't eat fish. | ✅ To this day, I don't eat fish. | The fixed phrase is "to this day". |

## 3. Speaking drill (45 min): voice mode with Claude

Tonight's drill is a **voice conversation with Claude**. You'll tell 3 complete stories and get corrections. See [Voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md) for setup.

1. **Set up (5 min).** Headphones on, notebook open, phone recording if you can. Start voice mode and paste or say this prompt:

```
I'm a B2 English learner practising casual storytelling. I'm going to tell you 3 short stories, one at a time, like we're friends chatting.
For each story:
1. Listen and react naturally like a friend would (short reactions, one or two follow-up questions).
2. After we chat about it, give me feedback in this order: (a) up to 3 mistakes, each with what I said and the natural American way to say it; (b) did I set the scene quickly, build suspense, use casual quotes like "I was like" / "she goes", and finish with a clear ending? Rate each one from 1 to 3.
3. Then say "Next story" and wait.
Keep your feedback short and spoken, not a long list. Use casual American English.
```

2. **Story 1 (8 min):** tonight's practice story from Exercise 4.3.
3. **Story 2 (8 min):** your Day 2 "something went wrong at work" story. Try to tell it faster than on Day 2.
4. **Story 3 (8 min):** your Day 3 misunderstanding story. Focus on the ending.
5. **Free chat (6 min):** ask Claude to tell *you* a short story, then react with phrases from [Week 1: reaction phrases](../../month-01-foundations/week-01-storytelling/day-2-reaction-phrases.md) and ask follow-ups.
6. **Write corrections (10 min).** In your notebook, write every correction Claude gave as: *what I said → better version*. Say each better version 3 times.

**Focus tonight:** don't stop to think about grammar while you're telling the story. Keep going. The corrections come after.

## 4. Review (15 min)

1. **Log 5 phrases** in your [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md): choose 3 ending phrases ("Needless to say…", "And the worst part?", "To this day…") and 2 corrections from Claude.
2. **Cover the log.** Say each correction's better version from memory, in a new sentence.
3. **Say Day 3's 5 quote phrases from memory**, each ending with a punchline. Example: "And she goes, 'Is that your car?' || It wasn't."
4. Write in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md) how many corrections you got and which story felt easiest.
