## Before you start

- Yesterday you explained your **job**. Tonight you explain one **project**, from the very beginning to the end, in about 3 minutes.
- In [Week 7 Day 1](../../month-02-speed/week-07-explaining/day-1-explaining-how-things-work.md) you learned sequence words for explaining how something works ("First you…, then…"). We won't repeat them. A project walkthrough is different: it's a **story with a purpose**, and the listener needs signposts so they know which part of the story they're in.
- Have your three project cards ready. Choose the one you know best for tonight.
- Tonight is **live conversation #1** of the week. Make sure you have a partner booked (see [live conversations](../../toolkit/live-conversations.md)). Backup: voice mode with Claude.

## Learning objectives

- Structure a walkthrough in five stages: starting point → goal → what we did → result → what I learned.
- Use signpost phrases so the listener always knows where they are.
- Zoom in on the interesting part and skip the boring parts ("fast-forward to…").
- Say clearly what **you** did versus what the **team** did.
- Stress new information in a demo ("THIS is the OLD screen").

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's focus: demo stress.** When people show something ("Here's the old version, and here's the new one"), they stress the words that point and contrast: THIS, THAT, OLD, NEW, BEFORE, AFTER, and the number that changed. Everything else is light and quick. This is the same rule as yesterday (new information gets the stress), but in demos the contrast is very strong.

| # | Sentence (CAPITALS = stressed) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | So THIS is the OLD checkout. | Point at "THIS", slight pause after "checkout". |
| 2 | And THIS is the NEW one. | "one" is old info, so it's quiet. |
| 3 | The old one had SEVEN steps. The new one has THREE. | Stress the numbers only. |
| 4 | Before, people dropped off RIGHT HERE. | "dropped off" → "dropt-off" |
| 5 | So we MOVED the payment to the END. | "to the" → "tuh thuh", very quick |
| 6 | And what happened was — completions went UP. | Pause after "was", then lift. |
| 7 | Not a LITTLE bit. Like TWENTY percent. | "twenty" → "twenny" |
| 8 | Now, the INTERESTING part was the TESTING. | "interesting" → "IN-trest-ing", three syllables |
| 9 | We tried it with FIVE people first. | "tried it with" → "try-di-dwith" |
| 10 | THREE of them got stuck on the SAME button. | "got stuck on" → "gah-stuck-on" |
| 11 | So we went BACK and changed the WORDING. | "went back" → "wen-back" |
| 12 | And THAT's basically how we got HERE. | Falling tone, "basically" quick. |

**Method:** speak-along twice with a whole clip, then pause-and-repeat the 2–3 hardest sentences. Then the 12 sentences above twice, pointing at an imaginary screen on THIS/THAT. See [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

**Clip to find:** a casual product demo or design case study. Search YouTube for "app redesign before and after walkthrough", "UX case study presentation", "product demo walkthrough startup", "design portfolio review walkthrough".

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Walking through a project

### Explanation

When someone says "Tell me about a project you worked on" — in an interview, at a meetup, or a new colleague over lunch — they want a short story they can follow. The big danger is that you start in the middle, list everything you did in order, and never say why it mattered. After a minute, the listener is lost.

Use **five stages**, and name each stage with a signpost so the listener can follow:

| Stage | Question it answers | Signpost examples | Time (in a 3-min version) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. Starting point | What was the situation and the problem? | "So the starting point was…", "The problem we had was…" | 30 sec |
| 2. Goal | What were you trying to achieve? | "The goal was to…", "What we wanted was…" | 15 sec |
| 3. What we did | The main steps — but only the interesting ones | "So first we…", "Where it got interesting was…", "Fast-forward a couple of weeks…" | 75 sec |
| 4. Result | What changed? A number, a reaction, a launch | "The end result was…", "And what happened was…" | 30 sec |
| 5. What I learned | What would you keep or change? | "The big lesson for me was…", "If I did it again, I'd…" | 30 sec |

**Three rules that make it sound natural:**

1. **Zoom in, zoom out.** Don't give every step equal time. Skip boring parts with "fast-forward…" or "long story short…" and slow down for the one moment that was surprising or difficult.
2. **"I" and "we".** Listeners want to know what *you* did. Use "we" for the team's work and "I" for your part: "We were redesigning checkout. My part was the payment screen." Don't hide behind "we" all the time, and don't steal the team's work with "I" all the time.
3. **Give one number or one reaction.** "Completions went up 20 percent" or "Support calls about it basically stopped" is much stronger than "It was good."

**Tense tip.** Tell the story in past simple ("we tested", "I changed"), but use present simple for things that are still true ("the app has about a million users", "the new flow is still live").

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So the starting point was… | you open the walkthrough | "So the starting point was a checkout that nobody liked." |
| The problem we had was… | you name the problem | "The problem we had was that people quit at the payment step." |
| The goal was to… | you state the goal | "The goal was to get more people through checkout without adding staff." |
| My part in it was… | you separate your role from the team's | "My part in it was the research and the payment screen." |
| So first we… | you begin the steps | "So first we watched about thirty session recordings." |
| Where it got interesting was… | you zoom in on the key moment | "Where it got interesting was the first test — nobody found the button." |
| Fast-forward (a couple of weeks)… | you skip time | "Fast-forward a couple of weeks, and we had a working prototype." |
| Long story short, … | you compress a long part | "Long story short, the engineers found a way to save the card details." |
| What we ended up doing was… | you describe the final solution | "What we ended up doing was moving payment to the very end." |
| The end result was… | you give the result | "The end result was a 20 percent jump in completed orders." |
| The big lesson for me was… | you reflect | "The big lesson for me was to test earlier, even with ugly sketches." |
| If I did it again, I'd… | you say what you'd change | "If I did it again, I'd involve the support team from day one." |
| Does that give you a sense of it? | you check and hand over | "So that's the project. Does that give you a sense of it?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: a full 3-minute walkthrough (to a new colleague at lunch)**

> A: So what were you working on before you joined this team?
> B: Oh, a checkout redesign for a food delivery app. So the starting point was this checkout that had seven steps, and the problem we had was that a lot of people just quit at the payment step. Like, one in three.
> A: Wow, that's a lot.
> B: Yeah. So the goal was to get more people through checkout without changing the prices or anything. My part in it was the research and then designing the payment screen. So first we watched a bunch of session recordings — basically videos of people using the app, no faces or anything — and we saw that people were getting surprised by the delivery fee right at the end.
> A: Oh, I hate that.
> B: Everybody hates that! So where it got interesting was the first test. We moved the fee to the top, and people still got stuck — but now on a different button. Turned out the button said "Proceed", and people didn't know if it meant "pay now" or not.
> A: Ha, OK.
> B: So we changed it to "Pay 24 dollars" — with the real amount. Fast-forward a couple of weeks, the engineers built it, we launched it to ten percent of users first, and the end result was about 20 percent more completed orders.
> A: That's huge.
> B: Yeah, we were pretty happy. The big lesson for me was that words matter as much as layout. If I did it again, I'd test the wording way earlier. So yeah, that's the project.

**Example 2: a short version (45 seconds, at a meetup)**

> A: What kind of stuff do you work on?
> B: Right now, onboarding for a fitness app. So the problem was people downloaded it and never opened it again. What we ended up doing was cutting the sign-up from five screens to two and letting people try a workout before making an account. Long story short, way more people came back on day two. The big lesson for me was: don't make people work before they get anything.

**Example 3: being asked about your role**

> A: So did you design the whole thing?
> B: No, no — it was a team of six. We had a PM, three engineers, a researcher and me. My part in it was the flows and the screens, and I ran the usability tests with the researcher. The engineers came up with the trick for saving card details, which honestly made the biggest difference.
> A: That's nice of you to say.
> B: It's true, though! Without that, my screens wouldn't have mattered much.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Starting with "So I opened Figma and made a frame…" | "So the starting point was a checkout people hated." | Start with the problem, not with your tools. The listener needs a reason to care. |
| "First we did this, then we did this, then we did this…" (ten steps) | "So first we… Where it got interesting was… Fast-forward…" | Equal time for every step is boring. Zoom in on one moment. |
| "We did everything." / "I did everything." | "We were redesigning checkout. My part was the payment screen." | Be clear and honest about your role. |
| "The result was good." | "The end result was about 20 percent more completed orders." | A number or a real reaction makes the result believable. |
| "The project is finished in March." | "The project finished in March." / "We launched it in March." | Finished events take past simple. |
| "What we ended up to do was…" | "What we ended up doing was…" | "End up" is followed by -ing. |

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

**Part A — 3/2/1 drill: one project walkthrough (30 min).** See [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

Prompt: **"Walk me through one project you're proud of, from the problem to the result."** Use the project card you chose tonight.

1. Record 3 minutes. Hit all five stages and use at least six signposts from the table.
2. Listen back (3 min). Mark: Did you say what *your* part was? Did you give a number or a reaction?
3. Record 2 minutes. Cut the boring middle with "fast-forward" or "long story short".
4. Record 1 minute. Starting point, the interesting moment, the result. Nothing else.
5. Now do the 2-minute version for a **second** project card, with no preparation (8 min including listening back).

**Part B — Live conversation #1 (40 min).** See [live conversations](../../toolkit/live-conversations.md).

**Who:** a colleague from another team, a friend who works in a different field, or a language exchange partner.

**Your mission:** get your partner to ask you about your work, and walk them through one project in 2–3 minutes. Then ask them to walk you through something they worked on (a project, a renovation, a trip they organised, a big event) and ask follow-up questions.

Things to try:
1. Open with a bridge from small talk: "Actually, I just finished this project at work that you might find funny…"
2. Use "My part in it was…" at least once.
3. Use "Where it got interesting was…" to zoom in.
4. Give one real number or reaction for the result.
5. When they walk you through theirs, ask "What was the hardest part?" and "What would you do differently?"

**Backup if your partner cancels:** voice mode with Claude ([voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md)). Paste this:

```text
Let's do a 30-minute casual voice conversation. You're a friendly new colleague from the marketing team, and we're having lunch. Start with small talk for a few minutes, then ask me what I worked on before. Let me walk you through a project for 2–3 minutes without interrupting, then ask me 3 natural follow-up questions, like a real curious person would. After that, tell me about a marketing campaign "you" ran and let me ask you questions. At the end, tell me: (1) did my walkthrough have a clear starting point, goal, result and lesson? (2) which signpost phrases did I use? (3) 3 grammar or word mistakes, with the corrected sentence. Keep your turns short and casual, American English.
```

After the conversation, write down two things that went well and one moment where you got lost or froze.
