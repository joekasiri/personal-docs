# Week 18 exercises: Explaining your work
[Week 18 overview](README.md) · [Day 1](day-1-your-job-for-outsiders.md) · [Day 2](day-2-walking-through-a-project.md) · [Day 3](day-3-explaining-trade-offs.md) · [Day 4](day-4-answering-why-questions.md) · [Day 5](day-5-success-and-failure-stories.md) · [Day 6](day-6-weekly-check.md)

**How to use this page.** Every exercise is done **out loud**, even the ones where you write first. Write only keywords, never full sentences, then speak. When an exercise says "record", record it and listen back once. Answer keys and model answers are at the end of each day's section. Look at them only *after* you've said your own answer out loud. Model answers are examples, not the only right answer: if yours is clear and natural, it's correct.

### Exercise 1.2: Three lengths
**Time:** 8 min · **Type:** record

1. Read the model below (a product designer at a banking app) once, out loud.
2. Now make your own three versions. Use keywords only, then record each one:
   - **One sentence** (5 seconds) — no job title, no jargon.
   - **30 seconds** — one-liner + a bridge ("You know when you…?") or an analogy + a tiny example.
   - **2 minutes** — all four parts + what you like or dislike about the job + a question back.
3. Do the 30-second version **three times**, each time for a different listener: (a) a 70-year-old relative, (b) a 12-year-old, (c) a doctor at a wedding.

**Model — one sentence:**
> "Basically, I design apps so they're easy to use."

**Model — 30 seconds:**
> "I'm a product designer at a bank. You know when you send money from your phone? Somebody decided every step of that — what you see, which button you press, what happens if you make a mistake. That's me. Like last month, I made it so you can see the fee before you send, not after."

**Model — 2 minutes:**
> "So I'm a product designer. Basically, I design the bank's app so it's easy to use. You know when you pay a bill on your phone and it's, like, three taps and you're done? Somebody worked out every one of those taps. Think of it as being an architect, but for software — the engineers are the builders, and I figure out what to build and how people will move through it.
> The part people don't see is how much of it isn't drawing. I spend a lot of time talking to customers, watching them use the app, and seeing where they get stuck. So, for example, we noticed older customers kept calling the bank because they couldn't find their statements. So I moved statements to the main screen and changed the name from 'Documents' to 'Statements'. Calls about it dropped a lot.
> What I love is that moment when someone uses something you made and doesn't even notice it — because it just works. What I don't love is the meetings, honestly. Anyway, that's the job in a nutshell. What do you do?"

## Day 2 exercises

### Exercise 2.1: Put the walkthrough in order
**Time:** 5 min · **Type:** out loud

These 10 sentences are a project walkthrough, but they're mixed up. Put them in order (write the letters), then read the whole walkthrough out loud as one smooth story, stressing the new information.

- A. "Where it got interesting was the first test — people didn't trust the robot icon at all."
- B. "So the starting point was a support page that nobody could find anything on."
- C. "The big lesson for me was that people need to know they can reach a human."
- D. "My part in it was designing the chat window and running the tests."
- E. "The end result was about 30 percent fewer support emails in the first month."
- F. "The goal was to help people solve simple problems without emailing us."
- G. "Fast-forward a couple of weeks, and we had a version that was ready to test."
- H. "So we added a 'Talk to a person' button right at the top, and suddenly people relaxed."
- I. "So first we read through about 200 old support emails to see what people asked most."
- J. "If I did it again, I'd show the 'Talk to a person' option from day one."

**Model walkthrough (a design system project):**
> "So the starting point was that our app had, like, fourteen different kinds of buttons. Every team was making their own, so nothing matched and engineers were rebuilding the same stuff over and over. The goal was to have one shared set of pieces that every team could use. My part in it was leading the design side — I worked with two engineers who built the coded versions.
> So first we did an audit — basically, we screenshotted every button, menu and form in the app and stuck them on one giant board. That was kind of shocking, honestly. Where it got interesting was getting the teams to actually use it. People liked their own buttons! So what we ended up doing was making the shared ones faster to use than making new ones — just drag and drop. Fast-forward about four months, and most teams had switched.
> The end result was that new screens took about a third less time to build, and the app finally looked like one product. The big lesson for me was that a design system isn't really a design problem — it's a people problem. If I did it again, I'd involve the other teams from week one instead of showing them a finished thing."

## Day 3 exercises

### Exercise 3.1: Find the trade-off
**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

For each decision, say **one or two sentences** with the choice, the cost and the gain. Use a different phrase each time: "The downside is…, but…", "We gave up X to get Y", "It's a trade-off between X and Y", "The cost of that is…", "For us, X mattered more than Y".

1. You launched a new feature on mobile only, not on the website.
2. You removed three rarely used settings from the app.
3. You used a ready-made date picker instead of designing a custom one.
4. You made the "Delete account" button harder to find, but added a clear help article about it.
5. You added a confirmation screen before payments.
6. You designed for small phones first, so the desktop version looks a bit empty.
7. You delayed a launch by two weeks to fix accessibility problems.
8. You chose one big, bold button on the home screen instead of six smaller options.

### Exercise 3.2: "If we'd gone with…" transformations
**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

Change each sentence into a sentence about the road not taken. Use "If we'd…, we would have…" (or "we'd have…", "we might have…"). Say each one out loud twice: once carefully, once fast ("if we'd" → "if weed", "would have" → "woulda").

1. We didn't build our own chat tool, so we launched in a week.
2. We didn't test with real users, so we missed the confusing button.
3. We chose the simple design, so new users understood it quickly.
4. We didn't wait for the new brand colours, so we finished on time.
5. We launched on a Tuesday, not a Friday, so the team was there to fix bugs.
6. We didn't add dark mode, so the release was three weeks earlier.
7. We listened to the support team, so we found the real problem.
8. We picked the cheaper tool, so we couldn't customise the reports.
9. We didn't ask legal early, so we had to redo the sign-up screen.
10. We cut the feature list in half, so we hit the deadline.

### Exercise 3.3: Four-move trade-off, say it 3 ways
**Time:** 8 min · **Type:** record

The same trade-off sounds different to different listeners. For each scenario, record three short versions (45 seconds each):
- **(a) to an engineer** — practical, mention effort and technical cost
- **(b) to the CEO** — short, mention money, risk and customers
- **(c) to a friend** — casual, an everyday comparison

Use all four moves each time: choice → cost → gain → why it was worth it.

**Scenario 1:** You shortened sign-up from 6 screens to 2. You now ask for less information at the start, so the marketing team gets less data about new users.

**Scenario 2:** You chose to fix 40 small bugs this month instead of building the big new feature everyone was excited about.

**Scenario 3:** You made the app's text bigger by default for readability. Fewer things fit on each screen, so people scroll more.

### Answer key: Day 3

**Exercise 3.1 — model answers**
1. "We launched on mobile only. The downside is website users have to wait, but 80 percent of our users are on their phones, so they get it a month earlier."
2. "We gave up three settings to get a much simpler screen. A few power users complained, but most people never touched them."
3. "It's a trade-off between personality and speed. The ready-made picker is a bit boring, but it took a day instead of three weeks, and it already works with screen readers."
4. "The cost of that is people who want to leave have to look a bit harder. But the help article explains it clearly, so it's not a trap — and fewer people delete by accident."
5. "The downside is one more tap before paying, but people stopped sending money to the wrong person, so for us safety mattered more than speed."
6. "We gave up a bit of polish on desktop to get a really good phone experience, because that's where most people use it."
7. "It's a trade-off between speed and doing the right thing. We lost two weeks, but now people using screen readers can actually use it — and we avoid fixing it in a panic later."
8. "For us, clarity mattered more than choice. The cost of that is some features are one more tap away, but new users know exactly what to do first."

**Exercise 3.2 — answer key**
1. If we'd built our own chat tool, we wouldn't have launched in a week. / we would have taken months.
2. If we'd tested with real users, we would have found the confusing button.
3. If we'd gone with a more complex design, new users wouldn't have understood it so quickly.
4. If we'd waited for the new brand colours, we wouldn't have finished on time.
5. If we'd launched on a Friday, the team wouldn't have been there to fix the bugs.
6. If we'd added dark mode, the release would have been three weeks later.
7. If we hadn't listened to the support team, we would never have found the real problem.
8. If we'd picked the more expensive tool, we could have customised the reports.
9. If we'd asked legal early, we wouldn't have had to redo the sign-up screen.
10. If we hadn't cut the feature list in half, we would have missed the deadline.

**Exercise 3.3 — model answers for Scenario 1**
- **(a) Engineer:** "So we cut sign-up from six screens to two. The downside is we're not collecting job title and company size up front anymore, so those fields will be empty for new users. But it's way less validation logic for you, and more people actually get through. We can ask for the rest later, inside the app, when they've seen the value."
- **(b) CEO:** "We shortened sign-up to two screens. The cost is that marketing gets less data on day one. But completed sign-ups went up, and a customer we don't have gives us zero data. On balance, it's clearly worth it."
- **(c) Friend:** "You know when a shop makes you fill in a whole form just to get a loyalty card? We did that — and people just walked out. So now we only ask for your email. Marketing's a bit sad, but way more people sign up."

## Day 5 exercises

### Exercise 5.1: Fix the tone
**Time:** 7 min · **Type:** out loud

Each sentence has a tone problem: **bragging**, **too modest**, **hiding the failure** or **beating yourself up**. Name the problem, then say a better version out loud.

1. "Basically, I single-handedly saved the whole product."
2. "Oh, I didn't really do anything. It was all the team."
3. "There were some unexpected challenges during the launch phase."
4. "I'm so bad at my job. I always mess everything up."
5. "My redesign was a massive, incredible success, honestly the best in the company."
6. "Some mistakes were made with the client presentation."
7. "It was just luck, really. Anyone could have done it."
8. "It was a complete disaster and I'll never forget how stupid I was."
9. "I transformed the user experience completely."
10. "The numbers went down a bit, but it wasn't really anyone's fault."

### Exercise 5.2: Story builder — one success, one failure
**Time:** 13 min · **Type:** write keywords, then record

1. Fill in the grid with keywords for one real success and one real failure (or use a failure from the Day 5 lesson list).
2. Record the success story (about 90 seconds). Then record the failure story (about 90 seconds).
3. Check with the list below the grid. Re-record the one that missed more points.
4. Read the model stories out loud once, copying the pauses at ‖.

| Part | Success (keywords) | Failure (keywords) |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up | | |
| Turning point | | |
| Your part | | |
| Result | | |
| Lesson / change | | |

**Check:**
- Success: Did you say clearly what you did? Did you give a number? Did you name one specific person for credit? Did you mention a moment it almost failed?
- Failure: Did you own it in one sentence ("That one was on me")? Was the disaster part shorter than the lesson part? Did you end with "Now I always…" or "What I'd do differently is…"?

**Model success story:**
> "I'm actually pretty proud of this one. So we had a returns process that was, like, a nightmare — you had to print a form, find a box, call a number. My part in it was redesigning the whole thing in the app. It almost didn't happen, actually — the warehouse team said it was impossible. ‖ So we sat with them for a week, and ‖ turns out the only real problem was the label. So we made the app show a QR code instead, and you just show it at the post office. ‖ The end result was returns took about two minutes instead of fifteen, and complaints about returns basically disappeared. But I can't take all the credit — Marco on the warehouse team came up with the QR idea. What I took from it was: when someone says 'impossible', go and sit with them."

**Model failure story:**
> "So this was a couple of years ago. I designed a new feature to let people split bills with friends. Spent two months on it. Super proud. ‖ We launched it, and… ‖ almost nobody used it. Like, two percent. That one was on me — I'd only talked to people in our office, who all split bills all the time, and I never checked if normal customers actually wanted it. In hindsight, I fell in love with my idea. What I'd do differently is test the idea with ten real customers before designing a single screen. And now I always do a quick 'would you use this?' test first. It's saved me from at least three more bad ideas since."

### Answer key: Day 5

**Exercise 5.1 — model answers**
1. Bragging → "I led the redesign, and it helped turn things around — but it was a real team effort, especially our two engineers."
2. Too modest → "I designed the new flow, and the team built it. I'm pretty happy with how it turned out."
3. Hiding → "The launch broke on older phones, and we had to roll it back for a day."
4. Beating yourself up → "I made a mistake on that one. What I took from it was to double-check before sending."
5. Bragging → "My redesign went really well — we cut support calls by about a third."
6. Hiding → "I showed the client the wrong version in the presentation. Not my finest moment."
7. Too modest → "There was some luck, sure, but what made the difference was testing early."
8. Beating yourself up → "It was a bit of a disaster, to be honest. But now I always check the file before I share."
9. Bragging → "I redesigned the main flows, and people finish tasks a lot faster now."
10. Hiding → "The numbers went down a bit, and part of that was on me — I didn't test the new layout enough. We're fixing it now."
