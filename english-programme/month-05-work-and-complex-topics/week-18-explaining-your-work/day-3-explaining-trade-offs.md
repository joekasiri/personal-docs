## Before you start

- In [Week 7 Day 2: Explaining decisions](../../month-02-speed/week-07-explaining/day-2-explaining-decisions.md) you learned "We went with X because…", and in [Week 7 Day 3: Comparing things](../../month-02-speed/week-07-explaining/day-3-comparing-things.md) you learned "X is way better for…, but Y…". Tonight goes one step further. A **trade-off** is when you choose to *lose* something to *get* something. Explaining a trade-off means being honest about the cost, not just selling the choice.
- Yesterday you walked through a project. Tonight, find the trade-off hidden inside it. Every project has at least one: speed vs quality, simple vs flexible, what users want vs what the business needs.
- Have your three project cards ready.

## Learning objectives

- Name what you gave up and what you got, in one clear sentence.
- Admit a downside honestly without sounding unsure of your decision.
- Explain why the trade-off was worth it, with a reason the listener cares about.
- Talk about the option you didn't choose ("If we'd gone with…, we would have…").
- Use contrast stress: "It's FASTER, but it's LESS flexible."

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's focus: contrast stress.** When you compare two sides of a trade-off, both sides get a stress, and the voice often goes **up** on the first side and **down** on the second. The small word "but" is almost always light and quick: "b't". This sound pattern tells the listener "here are two sides" before they even understand all the words.

| # | Sentence (CAPITALS = stressed, ↗ up, ↘ down) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | It's FASTER ↗, but it's LESS flexible ↘. | "but it's" → "b'dits" |
| 2 | We gained SPEED ↗, we lost a bit of CONTROL ↘. | No "but" — the contrast is in the melody only. |
| 3 | The UPSIDE is it's cheap. The DOWNSIDE is it breaks. | Stress UP and DOWN, not "side". |
| 4 | It's not PERFECT, but it's GOOD enough for NOW. | "good enough" → "goodinuff" |
| 5 | We could've made it PRETTIER. We chose to make it CLEARER. | "could've" → "coulda" |
| 6 | SHORT term, it's more work. LONG term, it saves us TIME. | Two pairs of contrast. |
| 7 | It was a trade-off between SIMPLE and POWERFUL. | "trade-off between" → "trade-off b'tween" |
| 8 | If we'd gone with the OTHER option, we'd still be BUILDING it. | "we'd still be" → "weed still be" |
| 9 | USERS wanted more options. The DATA said fewer. | Strong contrast on USERS / DATA. |
| 10 | Honestly? It cost us a MONTH. But it was WORTH it. | Pause after "MONTH". |

**Method:** speak-along twice with a whole clip, pause-and-repeat the 2–3 hardest sentences, then read the 10 sentences above twice, drawing the up and down arrows in the air with your hand. See [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

**Clip to find:** a designer or engineer comparing two options out loud. Search YouTube for "design decisions trade-offs talk", "why we chose X over Y engineering", "product manager explains trade-offs", "pros and cons tech choice explained".

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Explaining trade-offs

### Explanation

Non-native speakers often explain decisions as if the decision was perfect: "We chose the simple design because it's better." Listeners, especially experienced colleagues, don't believe "perfect". They trust you more when you show you **saw the cost** and still chose it.

A clear trade-off explanation has four moves:

1. **The choice:** what you went with. "We went with a single long page instead of five steps."
2. **The cost:** what you gave up. "The downside is it looks a bit overwhelming at first."
3. **The gain:** what you got. "But people can see everything they need to fill in, so nobody gets surprised at the end."
4. **The reason it was worth it:** connect to what matters (users, money, time, risk). "And surprises were the main reason people quit, so for us that was the bigger problem."

Optional fifth move: **the road not taken.** "If we'd gone with the five steps, we'd have had a nicer first screen, but more people dropping out halfway."

**Common trade-offs in product work** (useful for everyone, not only designers):

| Trade-off | In plain words |
|---|---|
| speed vs quality | launch sooner with fewer features, or later and more polished |
| simple vs flexible | easy for most people, or more options for power users |
| short term vs long term | quick fix now, or a proper solution that takes longer |
| user wants vs business needs | what people ask for, or what keeps the company alive |
| consistency vs a special case | use the standard component, or design something custom |
| build vs buy | make it ourselves, or pay for a tool that already exists |

**Tone tip.** Say the downside in a calm, normal voice — not quietly, not apologetically. A downside is a fact, not a confession. The confident move is: say the cost clearly, then say why it's worth it.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The downside is…, but… | you name the cost, then the gain | "The downside is it takes longer to build, but it'll work on every screen." |
| The upside is… | you name the gain first | "The upside is people can start right away." |
| It's a trade-off between X and Y. | you name the two sides | "It's a trade-off between speed and polish." |
| We gave up X to get Y. | you state the trade clearly | "We gave up some flexibility to get a much simpler screen." |
| The cost of that is… | you explain what a choice costs | "The cost of that is a few power users will be annoyed." |
| It's not perfect, but… | you admit limits but defend it | "It's not perfect, but it's way better than what we had." |
| For us, X mattered more than Y. | you explain the priority | "For us, fewer support calls mattered more than a pretty first screen." |
| If we'd gone with…, we would have… | you describe the option you didn't choose | "If we'd gone with the custom build, we would have launched in summer, not spring." |
| Short term… long term… | you compare time frames | "Short term, it's more work. Long term, it saves everyone time." |
| We decided we could live with… | you accept a known cost | "We decided we could live with the slower load time." |
| On balance, it was worth it. | you conclude | "We lost two weeks, but on balance, it was worth it." |
| What we're watching is… | you show you're tracking the risk | "What we're watching is whether experts complain about the missing shortcuts." |

### Examples

**Example 1: explaining a trade-off to a curious friend**

> A: So why does your app make you do everything on one long page now? It used to be steps.
> B: Ha, you noticed! Yeah, so it's a trade-off between looking simple and being predictable. With steps, the first screen looks super easy, but then you get surprised later — like, "Wait, you need my ID?" And that's where people were quitting.
> A: Right.
> B: So we went with one long page. The downside is it looks a bit scary at first, I'll admit that. But people can see everything up front, so no surprises. And for us, fewer people quitting mattered more than a pretty first screen.
> A: Did it work?
> B: Yeah, more people finish now. What we're watching is whether people on small phones hate the scrolling. So far, it's OK.

**Example 2: a colleague challenges you**

> A: I'm not sure about using the standard dropdown here. It looks kind of boring.
> B: Totally fair. We could make something custom. The cost of that is maybe two extra weeks of engineering, plus it won't match the other forms. So we gave up a bit of personality to get consistency and speed.
> A: Hmm.
> B: It's not perfect, but if we'd gone custom, we would have pushed the launch to next month. We decided we could live with "boring" for now — and if people actually struggle with it, we'll revisit.
> A: OK, that's reasonable.

**Example 3: explaining build vs buy to a non-tech manager**

> Manager: Why are we paying for a chat tool instead of building our own?
> B: Short term, building it ourselves looks cheaper. But long term, we'd have to keep fixing it, adding features, dealing with security. The upside of buying is it works next week. The downside is we can't change everything we want, and we pay every month.
> Manager: So what's the call?
> B: On balance, buying is worth it. For us, getting support chat live before the busy season matters more than having it exactly our way.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "We chose the long page because it's better." | "We chose the long page. The downside is it looks scary, but there are no surprises." | "Better" with no cost sounds naive. Show the trade. |
| "The disadvantage of this solution is that…" | "The downside is…" | "Disadvantage" sounds written and formal. "Downside" is natural speech. |
| "If we chose the other option, we would have…" | "If we'd gone with the other option, we would have…" | Past situations that didn't happen need the third conditional: "if we'd + past participle". |
| "Sorry, it's not perfect, I know, sorry…" | "It's not perfect, but it's way better than what we had." | Apologising makes the decision sound wrong. Admit it once, calmly. |
| "We sacrificed the flexibility for gaining the simplicity." | "We gave up some flexibility to get simplicity." | Keep it short: "give up X to get Y". |
| "Pros: fast. Cons: expensive." (said like a list) | "It's fast, but it's expensive." | In speech, join both sides with "but" and contrast stress. |

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

Mixed drills built around tonight's lesson. Record everything. Tools: [random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md), [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

**Step 1 — Warm-up: everyday trade-offs (12 min).** These are random topics with a trade-off inside. Talk 1.5 minutes on each, no preparation. Name the cost and the gain each time.

1. Living in the city centre vs the suburbs
2. Buying a cheap phone every two years vs an expensive one every five
3. Working from home vs working in the office
4. Taking a direct flight vs a cheaper flight with a stop
5. Cooking at home every night vs ordering food
6. A job with a big salary vs a job with a short commute
7. Learning one language well vs two languages a bit

Pick any 6 (shuffle them; don't choose your favourites).

**Step 2 — 3/2/1: the trade-off in your project (25 min).** See [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

Prompt: **"Tell me about one decision in your project where you had to give something up. What did you lose, what did you get, and was it worth it?"**

1. Record 3 minutes. Use all four moves plus the road not taken.
2. Listen back (2 min). Did you say the downside in a calm, normal voice?
3. Record 2 minutes, faster.
4. Record 1 minute: choice, cost, gain, "on balance".
5. Repeat the 1-minute version with a different project card (5 min including listening).

**Step 3 — Role-plays: someone questions your trade-off (25 min).** Read the role card, then speak your side for 2–3 minutes. Answer the other person's objections out loud, as if they're interrupting you. Use last week's [pushing back politely](../week-17-meetings/day-3-pushing-back-politely.md) phrases where they fit.

| Role card | Situation | The other person says… | Your trade-off to defend |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | A senior engineer in a design review | "Why are we launching without dark mode? Users have been asking for it for a year." | You chose to launch the new home screen in 3 weeks without dark mode, instead of in 8 weeks with it. |
| B | A sales manager | "Our biggest client wants a custom report screen. Why can't design just make it?" | You chose one flexible report screen for all clients instead of custom screens per client. |
| C | Your flatmate (non-work, to practise everyday trade-offs) | "Why did you book the 6 a.m. flight? That's crazy." | It was 200 dollars cheaper and you get a full extra day at your destination; the cost is waking up at 3:30. |

For each card: (1) choice, (2) cost said calmly, (3) gain, (4) why it was worth it for *this* person, (5) what you're watching or when you'll revisit.

**Step 4 — Listen back and count (8 min).** Pick your best role-play. Count your 3 numbers ([the 3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md)). Write them in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md). Note how many times you said "but" with a clear contrast before and after.
