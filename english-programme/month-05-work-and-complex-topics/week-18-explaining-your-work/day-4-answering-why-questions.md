## Before you start

- In [Week 6 Day 4: Giving reasons and examples](../../month-02-speed/week-06-opinions/day-4-giving-reasons-and-examples.md) you learned to give *one* reason and an example. Tonight is about what happens next: the person asks "why?" again… and again. A chain of "why?" questions is normal in design reviews, interviews and with curious friends. It's also the moment many learners freeze, because each answer has to go deeper.
- Yesterday's trade-offs are perfect material: every trade-off invites "But why did that matter more?"
- Tonight's drill is **voice mode with Claude**. Check your setup in [voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md). Have your three project cards nearby, but don't read from them.

## Learning objectives

- Answer "why?" with a short answer first, then the longer reason.
- Go one level deeper each time: from what you did → the reason → the real goal underneath.
- Point to evidence (what you saw, heard, measured) instead of just your opinion.
- Be honest about practical reasons (time, budget, a boss's decision) without sounding defensive.
- Stress the real reason in your sentence: "because of TIME, not money".

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's focus: stress on the reason.** When you answer "why?", the listener is waiting for one word or idea: the reason. Put the strongest stress on it. If you're correcting what they assumed, stress both sides ("Not because it's CHEAPER — because it's FASTER"). The words "because" and "it's" become very quick: "cuz", "s".

| # | Sentence (CAPITALS = stressed) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Mostly because of TIME. | "because of" → "cuzza" |
| 2 | Not because it's CHEAPER — because it's FASTER. | Correcting an assumption: two strong stresses. |
| 3 | The short answer is: USERS asked for it. | Pause after "is". |
| 4 | The LONGER answer is a bit more complicated. | "a bit more" → "a bidda more" (flap T) |
| 5 | We SAW it in the testing. Three out of FIVE people got stuck. | Stress the evidence. |
| 6 | Honestly? Part of it was the DEADLINE. | Rising "Honestly?" then flat. |
| 7 | The REAL reason is we didn't want MORE support calls. | "didn't want" → "didn' want" (T disappears) |
| 8 | Because if people get LOST, they just LEAVE. | "get lost" → "ge-lost" |
| 9 | That's a GOOD question, actually. I hadn't thought about it like THAT. | "good question" said warmly, not as a delay. |
| 10 | Why does that MATTER? Because it's their MONEY. | Asking your own why, then answering. |
| 11 | It wasn't MY call, to be honest — but I AGREED with it. | Contrast MY / AGREED. |
| 12 | So, at the end of the day, it's about TRUST. | "at the end of the day" → "athee-endathuh-day", quick |

**Method:** speak-along twice with a whole clip, pause-and-repeat the 2–3 hardest sentences, then the 12 sentences above twice. See [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

**Clip to find:** an interview where a founder or designer is asked tough "why" questions. Search YouTube for "founder interview why did you", "design critique session real", "product manager interview why questions", "tech CEO hard questions interview".

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Answering "why?" questions

### Explanation

When someone asks "why?" several times in a row, they're usually not attacking you. They're trying to find the **real reason** under your decision. Designers even have a method for this called "the five whys". But when it happens in English, it feels like pressure, and the brain empties.

The trick is to know that each "why?" moves you **one level down**:

| Level | What you talk about | Example |
|---|---|---|
| 1. What | What you did | "We moved the price to the top of the page." |
| 2. Reason | The direct reason | "Because people were surprised by the fee at the end." |
| 3. Evidence | How you know | "We saw it in the recordings — people scrolled back up, then quit." |
| 4. Goal | Why that matters | "Because surprises make people feel tricked, and then they don't trust us." |
| 5. Big picture | What the company or user really needs | "At the end of the day, it's about trust. If they don't trust us with ten dollars, they won't trust us with their card." |

So when you hear "why?" again, don't panic. Just go to the next level.

**Short answer first.** Under pressure, start with a short, direct answer in five words or fewer, then add the longer reason. "Mostly because of time. So…" This gets you talking immediately and shows confidence. The pattern "The short answer is… The longer answer is…" is gold when the real reason is complicated.

**Evidence beats opinion.** "I think it's better" invites another "why?". "We saw…", "People told us…", "The numbers showed…" usually ends the chain.

**Honest practical reasons are fine.** Sometimes the real reason is a deadline, a budget, or a boss. Say it plainly and add what you think: "Honestly, part of it was the deadline. But I think it was also the right call, because…". Pretending everything was a perfect design decision sounds fake.

**Not defensive.** Defensive sounds like: "Because I said so", "Well, I'm the designer", speaking faster and louder, or a long nervous list. Calm sounds like: a short answer, one piece of evidence, and sometimes "That's a fair question."

**If you don't know:** say so and say what you'd do. "I don't actually know — that's something we should check." (More on this in [Week 11 Day 3](../../month-03-spontaneity/week-11-never-stopping/day-3-talking-when-youre-unsure.md).)

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| The short answer is… | you start with a quick, direct reason | "The short answer is: people asked for it." |
| The longer answer is… | you go deeper after the short answer | "The longer answer is that the old flow was built for desktop." |
| Mostly because… / Partly because… | you show there's more than one reason | "Mostly because of time, partly because of the budget." |
| We saw that… / We noticed… | you give evidence from research | "We noticed people scrolling back up before they quit." |
| The numbers showed… | you give data as evidence | "The numbers showed half of them left on that screen." |
| The real reason is… | you go to the deeper goal | "The real reason is we didn't want more support calls." |
| Why does that matter? Because… | you ask the next "why" yourself and answer it | "It's faster. Why does that matter? Because people pay on the bus." |
| At the end of the day, it's about… | you reach the big picture | "At the end of the day, it's about trust." |
| Honestly, part of it was… | you admit a practical reason | "Honestly, part of it was the launch date." |
| It wasn't my call, but… | the decision came from someone else | "It wasn't my call, but I can see why they made it." |
| That's a fair question. | you show calm before answering a challenge | "That's a fair question. Mostly because…" |
| I don't actually know — that's worth checking. | you hit the bottom and don't know | "Why do they use it at night? I don't actually know — that's worth checking." |

### Examples

**Example 1: four "whys" in a design review**

> PM: Why did you remove the "skip" button from onboarding?
> B: The short answer is: almost everyone was skipping it.
> PM: Why's that a problem?
> B: Because the people who skipped didn't set up their goals, and we noticed they used the app way less after the first week. Like, half as much.
> PM: OK, but why not just make onboarding shorter instead?
> B: That's a fair question. We actually did that too — it's down to three screens. But the numbers showed even a short onboarding gets skipped if there's a big skip button.
> PM: And why does it matter so much if they set goals?
> B: Because at the end of the day, it's about the first win. If people see progress in week one, they stay. If they don't, they're gone.

**Example 2: honest practical reason**

> Friend: Why does your app still look so old? No offence.
> B: Ha, none taken. Honestly, part of it was money. A full redesign costs months, and the company decided to spend that time on fixing payments first.
> Friend: Why payments?
> B: Because that's where people were actually losing money — and getting angry. It wasn't my call, but I agree with it. Nobody leaves an app because it looks old; they leave because their payment failed.

**Example 3: reaching the bottom**

> Interviewer: Why do you think users preferred the dark version?
> B: We saw it in the test — seven out of ten picked it.
> Interviewer: Why do you think they picked it?
> B: Mostly because they use the app at night, in bed.
> Interviewer: Why at night?
> B: Hmm, I don't actually know — that's worth checking. My guess is it's when they have time to plan their week, but that's just a guess. I'd want to ask a few of them.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| (silence for 5 seconds, then a long answer) | "Mostly because of time. So…" | Start with a 5-word answer to stop the freeze. |
| "Because it's better." | "Because we saw people getting stuck on the old one." | "Better" is an opinion; evidence ends the chain. |
| "Because… because… because the users, they…" | "The short answer is: users asked for it." | Repeating "because" sounds nervous. Use a ready-made opener. |
| "Why? Because I'm the designer." | "That's a fair question. Mostly because…" | Defensive answers make people push harder. |
| "It was the decision of my manager." | "It wasn't my call, but I can see why they made it." | The natural phrase is "my call". Add your view so you don't sound like you're blaming. |
| "I don't know." (end) | "I don't actually know — that's worth checking. My guess is…" | "I don't know" alone stops the conversation. Add a guess or a next step. |

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min): voice mode with Claude

Tonight Claude asks "why?" after **every** answer. See [voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md). Turn on voice mode, record the session if you can, and keep your project cards face down.

**Round 1 — Your projects, 25 min.** Paste this prompt:

```text
We're going to practise answering "why?" questions in a casual voice conversation. I'm a product designer and a B2 English learner. Ask me to tell you about a project I worked on. After EVERY answer I give, ask "why?" or a short variation ("Why's that?", "But why does that matter?", "How come?", "Why not just…?"). Keep going down at least 4 levels on each topic before moving to a new one. Be curious, not aggressive. Don't explain or correct anything during the round — just keep asking. Cover 3 different decisions from my projects. After about 25 minutes, stop and give me feedback: (1) did I start my answers with a short, direct answer? (2) did I give evidence or only opinions? (3) did I ever sound defensive? (4) my 5 most important grammar or vocabulary mistakes, with corrected sentences. Use natural American English.
```

**Round 2 — Tough reviewer, 25 min.** After the feedback, paste this:

```text
Now let's make it harder. You're a senior product director in a design review. You're polite but sceptical and busy. I'll present a decision from my work in about 1 minute. Then ask "why?" after every answer, and sometimes challenge me with "Why not just do X instead?" or "I'm not convinced — why?". If I give an opinion without evidence, push on it. Do 3 decisions. Then give the same feedback as before, plus: which "why?" made me pause the longest, and a model answer for it.
```

**Round 3 — Everyday whys, 15 min.** Paste this:

```text
Last round, more relaxed. You're a curious friend. Ask me about choices in my everyday life — where I live, my job choice, how I spend weekends, why I'm learning English, a recent purchase. Ask "why?" after every answer, at least 4 levels deep. Keep it fun and casual. After 15 minutes, tell me the 3 best phrases I used and 3 phrases I should start using.
```

**What to focus on in all rounds:**
- Start every answer with 5 words or fewer: "Mostly because of…", "The short answer is…".
- Use "We saw…" or "The numbers showed…" at least once per decision.
- When you hit the bottom, say "I don't actually know — that's worth checking" instead of freezing.
- Keep your voice calm and your speed normal when the reviewer pushes.

**After the session (5 min):** write Claude's corrections in your [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md) as full, correct sentences. Say each corrected sentence out loud 3 times.
