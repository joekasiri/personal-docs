## Before you start

- You already know the basics of job talk from [Week 4 Day 5: Talking about your job](../../month-01-foundations/week-04-vocabulary/day-5-talking-about-your-job.md): what you do, a normal day, a recent problem. Tonight is **not** a repeat. Tonight is about the listener: how to explain the same job to a grandparent, a ten-year-old, a lawyer and a taxi driver, so each one understands and stays interested.
- Have your three project cards from the [week overview](README.md#before-you-start) next to you.
- Open your recording app and the [random topics](../../toolkit/random-topics.md) list.

## Learning objectives

- Explain your job at three lengths: one sentence, 30 seconds, 2 minutes.
- Replace work jargon with plain words and everyday comparisons (analogies).
- Use a concrete example from a product the listener already uses.
- Notice whether the listener is interested and go deeper or wrap up.
- Stress the new information in each sentence so the key idea stands out.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**This week's feature: stress on new information.** In English, the word that carries **new** information gets the strongest stress. Old information (something already said, or obvious) is squashed and quick. Speakers in product demos do this all the time: the first time they say a word, it's loud and long; the second time, it's quiet. If you stress everything the same, people can't tell what's important.

Tonight's focus is the stress in *introductions*: the moment someone says what they do.

| # | Sentence (CAPITALS = stressed, new info) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | I'm a PRODUCT designer. | "designer" is expected after "I'm a…", so "PRODUCT" carries the news. |
| 2 | I design the SCREENS you see in an APP. | "you see in an" → "ya see inan" |
| 3 | Not the LOOK so much — more how it WORKS. | Contrast: two stresses, a small pause at the dash. |
| 4 | So, you know when you order FOOD on your phone? | "you know when" → "y'know when" |
| 5 | Somebody DECIDED where that button GOES. That's ME. | Pause before "That's ME". |
| 6 | I work for a company that makes BANKING apps. | "that makes" → "thet makes", "banking" → "bankin" |
| 7 | My job is basically to make it less CONFUSING. | "basically" → "basic-lee", quick |
| 8 | I TALK to users, I SKETCH ideas, and then I TEST them. | Three stresses, same rhythm, like a list. |
| 9 | It's kinda like being an ARCHITECT, but for SOFTWARE. | "kinda like" → "kinda-lie" |
| 10 | The engineers BUILD it; I figure out WHAT to build. | Contrast between BUILD and WHAT. |
| 11 | Honestly? Most of my day is MEETINGS. | Rising "Honestly?", then a drop. |
| 12 | But the FUN part is watching someone USE it. | "watching someone" → "watchin' someone" |

**Method:** speak-along with a whole clip (no pausing) twice. Then pause-and-repeat only the 2–3 sentences you found hardest. Then read the 12 sentences above out loud twice, exaggerating the CAPITALS. See [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

**Clip to find:** someone explaining their tech job simply, in a casual voice. Search YouTube for "what does a product designer do explained", "explain my job to my parents tech", "UX designer day in the life", "I explain my job to a kid". Choose a speaker who is chatting, not reading a script.

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Your job for outsiders

### Explanation

When an outsider asks "So what do you do?", most designers make one of two mistakes. They either give a job title that means nothing ("I'm a senior product designer on the growth squad") or they explode into jargon ("I do end-to-end UX for onboarding funnels and conversion"). The listener nods, feels stupid, and changes the topic.

A good explanation for outsiders has four parts. You don't always need all four; it depends on how interested they are.

1. **The plain one-liner.** No job title, no jargon. Say what problem you solve for normal people. "I design apps so they're easy to use."
2. **The bridge to their life.** Connect to something they already use. "You know when you're paying for something online and it's really smooth? Somebody designed that."
3. **The comparison (analogy).** "It's kind of like being an architect, but for apps. The engineers are the builders."
4. **One concrete example.** A real, small thing you did. "Last month, people kept getting stuck on one screen when signing up, so I redesigned it and now way more people finish."

Then **check the listener**. If they ask a follow-up, go deeper. If their eyes move away, wrap up with a short ending and ask them something back ("Anyway, that's me. What about you?").

**Three lengths.** Prepare your job at three lengths and practise them until they're automatic:

| Length | When | What's in it |
|---|---|---|
| One sentence (5 seconds) | A quick hello, a noisy bar, someone being polite | The plain one-liner |
| 30 seconds | A normal "what do you do?" | One-liner + bridge or comparison + a tiny example |
| 2 minutes | Someone is genuinely curious, an interview, a dinner | All four parts + what you love or hate about it + a question back |

**Jargon swap.** Every field has words outsiders don't know. Before you speak, translate yours:

| Jargon | Plain English |
|---|---|
| user research | talking to the people who use the app, watching them use it |
| prototype | a fake, clickable version of the app to test ideas |
| user flow / funnel | the steps someone goes through, like sign up → pay → done |
| stakeholders | the people in the company who care about the result |
| conversion | how many people actually finish what they started (like buying) |
| iterate | try something, see what happens, fix it, try again |
| ship / launch | put it out so real people can use it |
| design system | a shared box of ready-made pieces (buttons, colours) so every screen matches |

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Basically, I… | you start the plain one-liner | "Basically, I make apps easier to use." |
| You know when you…? Well, … | you build a bridge to their life | "You know when you book a flight on your phone? Well, someone designed every step of that." |
| It's kind of like… but for… | you give an analogy | "It's kind of like being an architect, but for software." |
| Think of it as… | another way into an analogy | "Think of it as the person who decides how the shop is laid out, but online." |
| In plain English, … | you realise you just used jargon | "We do user research — in plain English, I watch people use the app and see where they get stuck." |
| The short version is… | you want to keep it brief | "The short version is: I make the screens." |
| So, for example, … | you give one concrete thing you did | "So, for example, last month I redesigned the sign-up so fewer people quit halfway." |
| The part people don't see is… | you show a surprising side of the job | "The part people don't see is how much time I spend just talking to people." |
| I'm the person who… | you describe your role in a team | "I'm the person who figures out what the engineers should build." |
| Not so much X — more Y | you correct a common belief | "Not so much making it pretty — more making it work." |
| Does that make sense, or am I being too nerdy? | you check the listener with humour | "I make the flows smoother. Does that make sense, or am I being too nerdy?" |
| Anyway, that's the job in a nutshell. | you wrap up and hand back | "Anyway, that's the job in a nutshell. What do you do?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: at a party, someone polite (30 seconds)**

> A: So what do you do?
> B: I'm a product designer. Basically, I design apps so they're easy to use. You know when you order food on your phone and it just… works? Somebody decided where every button goes. That's my job — but for a banking app.
> A: Oh, cool. So you, like, make it look nice?
> B: Not so much the look — more how it works. Like, how many steps it takes to send money, what happens if you make a mistake. That kind of thing.
> A: Huh. I never thought about that.
> B: Yeah, nobody does when it's good. That's kind of the point. Anyway, what about you?

**Example 2: explaining to a grandparent (slow, analogy-heavy)**

> Grandma: I still don't understand what you do at that company.
> B: OK, think of it like this. You know the bank branch near your house? Somebody decided where the counter goes, where you wait, where the signs are, so you don't get lost.
> Grandma: Yes.
> B: I do that, but for the bank on the phone. When you open the app, I decided what you see first, where the button is to pay your bills, and what happens if you press the wrong thing.
> Grandma: So you're like the person who puts up the signs.
> B: Exactly! A digital sign person. And I watch people use it, and when they get confused, I fix it.

**Example 3: a curious engineer friend from another industry (2 minutes, deeper)**

> A: Wait, so how's a product designer different from a graphic designer?
> B: Good question. A graphic designer mostly works on how things look — logos, posters, ads. I care about that too, but the part people don't see is the thinking before the screens. So, for example, right now we've got a problem where people start signing up and then just… leave. Like 40 percent of them.
> A: Why?
> B: That's my job — figuring out why. So I talk to users, I watch recordings of people using the app, I sketch some ideas, then I make a prototype — in plain English, a fake clickable version — and I test it with five or six people before the engineers build anything.
> A: So you're kind of like a detective.
> B: Ha, yeah, a detective who also draws. Honestly, it's like 30 percent design and 70 percent figuring out what the actual problem is. Does that make sense, or am I being too nerdy?
> A: No, that makes sense. It sounds more interesting than I thought.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "I'm a senior product designer in the growth squad." (and nothing else) | "I'm a product designer. Basically, I make our app easier to sign up for." | A job title alone tells outsiders nothing. Add the plain one-liner. |
| "I optimise conversion in onboarding funnels." | "I help more people actually finish signing up." | Jargon makes people feel stupid. Translate it. |
| "I make the design of the app." | "I design the app." / "I work on the design of the app." | "Make the design" sounds translated. Use "design" as a verb. |
| "It's like an architect for the software." | "It's kind of like being an architect, but for software." | The natural analogy pattern is "like being a…, but for…". |
| Talking for 3 minutes when they asked politely in a noisy bar | One sentence, then "What about you?" | Match the length to the listener's interest. |
| "Do you understand?" | "Does that make sense?" / "Am I making sense?" | "Do you understand?" sounds like a teacher testing them. Put the responsibility on you. |

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

Mixed drills built around tonight's lesson. Record everything. Tools: [random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md), [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

**Step 1 — Warm-up: random topics (10 min).** Pick 4 topics without looking from [random topics](../../toolkit/random-topics.md). Talk 1.5 minutes on each, no preparation. If you get a topic you know nothing about, use the "talking when you're unsure" phrases from [Week 11 Day 3](../../month-03-spontaneity/week-11-never-stopping/day-3-talking-when-youre-unsure.md) and keep going.

**Step 2 — 3/2/1: "My job, for someone who has never used an app" (25 min).** Imagine you're talking to someone who has never owned a smartphone (a person from 1980, if it helps). Explain what you do and why it matters.
1. Record 3 minutes. Use all four parts: one-liner, bridge, analogy, example.
2. Listen back for 2 minutes. Note one place where you used jargon.
3. Record 2 minutes. Same content, faster, with no jargon.
4. Record 1 minute. Keep the one-liner, the analogy and the example only.
5. Rest 2 minutes, then repeat the 1-minute version once more without looking at anything.

**Step 3 — Role-plays: four outsiders (25 min).** For each role card, imagine the person in front of you. Speak for 2–3 minutes, answering the questions in the card out loud as if they asked them. Record each one.

| Role card | Who they are | What they ask you | Your challenge |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | Your neighbour's 10-year-old, who loves video games | "Do you make games? What do you actually do on the computer all day?" | Use a game analogy. No word longer than three syllables. |
| B | A lawyer at a wedding, slightly bored, polite | "Oh, design. So, like, websites?" | Correct the belief politely ("Not so much… more…"), keep it to 45 seconds, ask back. |
| C | A taxi driver who uses a ride-hailing app all day | "These apps, they change all the time. Is that you guys? Why do you keep moving the buttons?" | Use his app as the bridge. Explain why things change, without defending everything. |
| D | Your friend's dad, a retired factory engineer, very curious | "But how do you know your design is good? Who decides?" | Go to the 2-minute version: testing with users, numbers, the team. Use one analogy from factories. |

**Step 4 — Listen back and count (10 min).** Choose your best role-play recording. Count your 3 numbers ([the 3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md)): umms, pauses over 2 seconds, different connectors. Write them in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md). Write down one jargon word that slipped out and its plain-English swap.

## 4. Review (15 min)

Spaced review, out loud, using your [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md):

2. **Yesterday (3 min):** say your Week 17 Day 6 phrases out loud from memory, one sentence each.
3. **Last week (4 min):** [Week 17 Day 1: Giving updates](../week-17-meetings/day-1-giving-updates.md). Give a 30-second update about one of your three projects using last week's update phrases.
4. **Last month (3 min):** [Week 13 Day 1: Jumping in](../../month-04-real-life-pressure/week-13-group-conversations/day-1-jumping-in.md). Say three ways to jump into a group conversation, then use one to start explaining your job ("Oh, that reminds me — that's actually what I do…").

Fill in tonight's row in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).
