## Learning objectives

- Tell a work success story that shares credit and sounds confident, not arrogant.
- Tell a failure story that owns the mistake and ends with what you learned and changed.
- Use the "turning point" of the story to keep people listening.
- Laugh at a failure a little, so it's easy for people to hear.
- Stress the turning point and the result.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's focus: story stress at the turning point.** In a work story, two moments carry the news: the **turning point** (when something went wrong or suddenly worked) and the **result**. Good storytellers slow down, pause just before the turning point, and stress it hard. Then they deliver the result clearly and a bit lower.

| # | Sentence (CAPITALS = stressed, ‖ = short pause) | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | So we launched on a FRIDAY ‖ which, in hindsight, was a BAD idea. | "in hindsight" → "in HIND-site" |
| 2 | And on Saturday morning ‖ my phone just EXPLODED. | Pause, then a big stress. |
| 3 | Turns out the button didn't work on OLDER phones. | "Turns out" → "Tur-zout" |
| 4 | That one was totally on ME. | "totally" → "toe-dully" (flap T) |
| 5 | What I'd do DIFFERENTLY ‖ is test on REAL devices. | Stress the change. |
| 6 | And then ‖ out of NOWHERE ‖ the numbers started going UP. | Two pauses for drama. |
| 7 | I can't take all the CREDIT — the ENGINEERS did the hard part. | Contrast CREDIT / ENGINEERS. |
| 8 | We cut sign-up time IN HALF. | Stress the result, voice lower at the end. |
| 9 | Honestly, I was SUPER nervous about it. | "super" → long "SOO-per" |
| 10 | Looking back, it was the BEST thing that could've happened. | "could've" → "coulda" |
| 11 | The funny part is ‖ nobody even NOTICED for a week. | Smile in your voice. |
| 12 | So now I ALWAYS check that first. | Strong ALWAYS as the lesson. |

**Method:** speak-along twice with a whole clip, pause-and-repeat the 2–3 hardest sentences, then the 12 sentences above twice, really holding the pauses. See [shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

**Clip to find:** someone telling a work failure or success story, casually. Search YouTube for "my biggest mistake at work story", "startup failure story founder", "design fail story podcast", "tell me about a time you failed good answer".

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Success and failure stories

### Explanation

Work stories have a tone problem. In many cultures, it's rude to talk about your success, so people make it so small that it disappears ("It was nothing, the team did it"). Or they feel nervous and overdo it ("I completely transformed the product"). With failures, the opposite: people either hide them ("There were some challenges") or beat themselves up ("It was terrible, I'm so bad at this").

American work culture usually likes a middle path:

**Success stories → confident + generous.**
- Say clearly what *you* did and what changed. Use a number.
- Share credit with specific people ("the engineers figured out…"), not just "the team".
- Show it wasn't easy: mention the moment it almost didn't work.
- Don't use big words like "revolutionised". Let the result speak.

**Failure stories → own it + lesson + change.**
- Say clearly what went wrong and what your part was: "That one was on me."
- Keep the "bad" part short. Don't spend two minutes on the disaster.
- Spend most of the time on what you did next and what you **changed** afterwards.
- A little humour helps: "We launched on a Friday — rookie mistake."
- End with what you do now because of it. That ending turns a failure into a strength.

**A simple shape for both:**

| Part | Success story | Failure story |
|---|---|---|
| Set-up (1–2 sentences) | "So we had this problem…" | "So this was my first big launch…" |
| Turning point | "And then we tried… and it actually worked." | "And then on Saturday, it all broke." |
| Your part | "My part was…" | "That was on me — I didn't…" |
| Result | "We cut it in half." | "We fixed it in a day, but we lost some trust." |
| Lesson / change | "What I took from it was…" | "Now I always… / What I'd do differently is…" |

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I'm actually pretty proud of this one. | you introduce a success with confidence | "I'm actually pretty proud of this one — it was my first big project." |
| I can't take all the credit — … | you share credit generously | "I can't take all the credit — the engineers did the hard part." |
| What made the difference was… | you explain the key move in a success | "What made the difference was testing with real customers early." |
| It almost didn't happen, actually. | you add tension to a success | "It almost didn't happen, actually — we nearly cancelled it." |
| That one was on me. | you own a mistake clearly | "We missed the bug, and honestly, that one was on me." |
| In hindsight, … | you look back and see the mistake | "In hindsight, launching on a Friday was a bad idea." |
| It was a bit of a disaster, to be honest. | you name a failure with light humour | "The first version was a bit of a disaster, to be honest." |
| What I'd do differently is… | you show what you learned | "What I'd do differently is involve support from day one." |
| What I took from it was… | you state the lesson | "What I took from it was that small tests save big headaches." |
| Now I always… | you end with the change you made | "Now I always test on an old phone before launch." |
| Looking back, it was a good thing. | you find a positive side | "Looking back, it was a good thing — it changed how we work." |
| It wasn't my finest moment. | you admit a personal mistake with humour | "I sent the wrong file to the client. Not my finest moment." |

### Examples

**Example 1: a success story without bragging (at after-work drinks)**

> A: What's the project you're proudest of?
> B: Oh, I'm actually pretty proud of this one. So we had this sign-up flow that took, like, four minutes. People hated it. And my part was the redesign. It almost didn't happen, actually — the company nearly pushed it to next year.
> A: Why?
> B: Too risky, they said. So we did a tiny test with five percent of users first. And then, out of nowhere, the numbers just went up and up. We cut sign-up time in half.
> A: That's awesome.
> B: Yeah! But I can't take all the credit — our engineer, Priya, figured out how to skip the email check for most people, and that's what made the difference. What I took from it was: start small, and the scary project suddenly isn't scary.

**Example 2: a failure story, owned (job interview style but casual)**

> Interviewer: Tell me about a time something went wrong.
> B: Sure. So this was my first big launch. We released a new payment screen on a Friday afternoon — which, in hindsight, was a bad idea. And on Saturday morning, my phone just exploded. Turns out the "Pay" button didn't work on older Android phones.
> Interviewer: Ouch.
> B: Yeah. That one was on me — I'd only tested on my own phone and the latest models. We rolled it back in about two hours, but we lost a lot of orders that morning. What I'd do differently is test on real, older devices and never launch right before a weekend. And now I always do both. We actually made it a team rule.

**Example 3: a short, funny failure with a friend**

> A: Have you ever totally messed up at work?
> B: Oh, totally. Once I presented a whole design to the CEO, and halfway through I realised I'd shared the wrong screen — it was my shopping list.
> A: No way!
> B: Yeah, not my finest moment. Everyone laughed, though, and the CEO goes, "I see you need milk." Now I always close everything before I share my screen.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| "I revolutionised the whole onboarding." | "I redesigned onboarding, and we cut sign-up time in half." | Big words sound like bragging. A number is more convincing. |
| "It was nothing, really, it was all the team." | "I led the redesign, and I can't take all the credit — Priya solved the email problem." | Too much modesty makes your role disappear. Be clear, then generous. |
| "There were some challenges with the launch." | "The launch broke on older phones, and that one was on me." | Vague failures sound like hiding something. |
| "It was a terrible failure, I was so stupid." | "It was a bit of a disaster, to be honest. Here's what I changed." | Don't beat yourself up. Own it briefly and move to the lesson. |
| "From this I learned a lot of things." | "What I took from it was: test on old phones." | Name one specific lesson. |
| "If I would do it again, I would test more." | "If I did it again, I'd test more." / "What I'd do differently is test more." | No "would" in the "if" part. |

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

**Part A — 3/2/1 drill: one success, one failure (30 min).** See [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

Prompt 1: **"Tell me about a project at work that went really well, and what your part in it was."**
Prompt 2: **"Tell me about a time something went wrong at work, and what you changed afterwards."**

If you don't have a real failure you want to tell, use one of these and make it yours:
- You designed a feature that took two months, and when it launched, almost nobody used it.
- You misunderstood what a client wanted and showed them the wrong thing in a big meeting.
- You promised a deadline you couldn't meet and had to tell your manager the day before.

Steps:
1. Prompt 1: record 3 min → 2 min → 1 min (about 12 min with quick listening).
2. Prompt 2: record 3 min → 2 min → 1 min (about 12 min).
3. Listen to both 1-minute versions (6 min). Check: Did the success share credit with a specific person? Did the failure end with "Now I always…"?

**Part B — Live conversation #2 (40 min).** See [live conversations](../../toolkit/live-conversations.md).

**Who:** a colleague over coffee, a friend, a language exchange partner, or a meetup (a design or tech meetup is perfect tonight).

**Your mission:** trade work stories. Tell one success and one failure (or a funny mistake), and get your partner to tell you theirs.

Things to try:
1. Start the swap with: "What's the worst thing that's ever happened to you at work?" — then be ready to go first.
2. Say "That one was on me" or "It wasn't my finest moment" in your failure story.
3. Say "I can't take all the credit — …" with a specific name in your success story.
4. React to their stories with Week 1 reaction phrases ("No way!", "That's rough") and ask "What would you do differently?".
5. Use one "why?" follow-up on their story, Day 4 style: "Why do you think that happened?"

**Backup if your partner cancels:** voice mode with Claude ([voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md)). Paste this:

```text
Let's have a 30-minute casual voice conversation. You're a friendly designer I just met at a tech meetup, and we're chatting at the bar after the talks. Start with small talk, then steer toward work stories: ask me about the project I'm proudest of and the worst thing that's happened to me at work. Share your own (invented) success and failure stories too, so it's a real swap. React naturally and ask follow-up "why?" questions. At the end, tell me: (1) did my success story sound confident or like bragging? (2) did my failure story own the mistake and end with a lesson? (3) my 4 most important language mistakes, with corrected sentences. Casual American English, short turns.
```

After the conversation, write down the best story your partner told and one phrase they used that you want to steal.
