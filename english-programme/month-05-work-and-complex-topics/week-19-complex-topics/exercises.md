# Week 19 exercises: Complex topics
[Week 19 overview](README.md) · [Day 1](day-1-news-and-current-events.md) · [Day 2](day-2-tech-and-ai.md) · [Day 3](day-3-culture-and-differences.md) · [Day 4](day-4-money-and-life-choices.md) · [Day 5](day-5-balanced-views.md) · [Day 6](day-6-weekly-check.md)

These are the main-skill exercises for each night (20 minutes a day), plus the materials for the weekly check. Three rules:

1. **Always out loud.** Even the "write then say" exercises: write a few key words only, then say the full answer. Never read a full sentence from paper.
2. **Record when the exercise says so**, and listen back to at least one recording each night.
3. **Every story in these exercises is invented.** They're here so you can practise the *language* of news, tech, culture and money without worrying about whether the facts are right.

Check yourself with the answer key at the end of each day. The model answers are examples, not the only correct answers.

## Day 1 exercises

### Exercise 1.1: Headline to hook
**Time:** 5 min · **Type:** out loud

Each story below is written like a long, boring report. Turn each one into **one casual hook sentence** that you'd say to a friend, starting with "Did you see that thing about…?", "I was reading something about…" or "Have you heard about…?". Say each one out loud twice, the second time faster.

1. A city council has voted to plant 10,000 trees over the next five years in neighbourhoods that get very hot in summer.
2. A large supermarket chain has announced it will remove self-checkout machines from some of its stores after customer complaints.
3. Researchers have published a study suggesting that people who read fiction regularly are better at understanding other people's feelings.
4. A popular music streaming app is testing a feature that lets users hide songs they don't like from all their playlists.
5. A national rail company will let passengers bring bikes on all trains for free from next year.
6. A small town has started paying people to move there and work remotely, to bring young people back.
7. An airline is testing a rule that passengers in window seats board first.
8. A school district is starting classes one hour later for teenagers, after research on sleep.
9. A company has started giving employees a paid "moving day" off when they move to a new home.
10. Scientists say a new type of battery could let phones charge fully in five minutes, but it won't be in shops for years.

### Exercise 1.2: The five-step summary
**Time:** 10 min · **Type:** record

Tell each story below in **five steps**: hook, background, why it matters, what's next, hand over (your quick take + one question). Aim for about **1 minute** each. Use at least two of these: "So basically…", "For context…", "Apparently…", "From what I understand…", "The interesting part is…", "It's still kind of up in the air.", "My take is…". Record stories A and B, listen to one of them, then do C and D.

**Story A: The no-meeting Wednesday**
- A tech company of about 3,000 people banned all internal meetings on Wednesdays for three months.
- Before, people said they had "no time to do real work".
- Results: people reported less stress and finished more projects. Some managers said decisions got slower.
- The company will decide next month whether to keep it.

**Story B: The library of things**
- A city library now lends out tools, sewing machines, camping gear and board games, not just books.
- Background: the idea came from residents who said they bought things they only used once.
- It's been very popular, with waiting lists for some items. Some local shops worry about losing sales.
- The city may open three more next year.

**Story C: The quiet carriage**
- A train company made one carriage on every commuter train a "phone-free" zone: no calls, no videos with sound.
- Background: passengers complained about noise for years.
- The interesting part: the quiet carriage is now the most crowded, but some people feel it's hard to enforce.
- It's still a trial. They'll survey passengers in the summer.

**Story D: The four-hour game limit**
- A game company added an optional setting that reminds players to take a break after four hours.
- Background: parents' groups had asked for better tools.
- Some players think it's helpful; others think it's annoying and says the company is just protecting its image.
- Other game companies haven't said if they'll do the same.

### Exercise 1.3: Fact or opinion?
**Time:** 5 min · **Type:** out loud

For each sentence, decide: is it a **fact from a story** (F) or **the speaker's opinion** (O)? Then say it out loud with a natural lead-in. For facts, use "Apparently…", "From what I read…" or "So basically…". For opinions, use "My take is…", "Personally…" or "If you ask me…". If the sentence is too certain for something you only saw in a headline, also soften it.

1. The trial lasted six months.
2. The trial was a total waste of money.
3. About 200 people took part in the study.
4. The study is too small to mean anything.
5. The new bike lanes will definitely make the city safer.
6. The city is building 40 kilometres of new bike lanes.
7. Most of the shop owners were against it at first.
8. The shop owners were wrong to complain.
9. Nobody will ever use the new app.
10. The app was downloaded a million times in the first week.
11. The company is planning to open offices in three more cities.
12. Working from a café every day is better than working in an office.

4. "Have you heard about that music app thing where you can hide songs you hate?"
5. "Did you see they're letting people take bikes on trains for free?"
6. "Did you see that thing about a town paying people to move there?"
7. "Have you heard about the airline that's boarding window seats first?"
8. "I was reading something about schools starting an hour later for teenagers."
9. "Did you hear some company's giving people a day off when they move?"

For context, people had been complaining that they had no time to actually do their work. And apparently it kinda worked: people said they were less stressed and they finished more projects. 12. O: "Personally, I think working from a café is better than an office. For me, anyway."

### Exercise 2.1: One plain sentence
**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

Explain each thing below to someone who doesn't work in tech, like a grandparent or a friend who's a nurse. Use **one plain sentence** starting with "It's basically a… that…" or "It's basically when…". No technical words. Then add one short example: "So, like…". Say each one twice.

3. A design system
4. A chatbot on a company website
5. Two-factor authentication
6. The algorithm on a social media feed
7. A clickable prototype
8. Machine translation
9. A smart thermostat
10. An app update that "fixes bugs"

### Exercise 2.2: Move it on the certainty scale
**Time:** 7 min · **Type:** out loud

Say each prediction below three times, at three different levels of certainty: **80%** ("It'll probably…", "I'd be surprised if… didn't…"), **50%** ("It could go either way whether…") and **30%** ("I doubt…", "I'm not convinced…"). Then choose the level you really believe and add one reason.

1. Most people will use voice instead of typing to search for things.
2. Every design tool will have AI built in.
3. Online shopping will completely replace shopping malls.
4. Self-driving taxis will be normal in big cities.
5. People will have AI assistants that book their appointments and answer their emails.
6. Students will stop writing essays by hand.
7. Smartwatches will replace a lot of visits to the doctor.
8. People will get tired of screens and spend more time offline.

Then **correct these five sentences** and say the correct version out loud:

a. "I am using this app since two years."
b. "It made my work more easy and more fast."
c. "Surely AI will replace all the designers in five years."
d. "I'm not convinced it will to happen."
e. "I'd be surprised if it doesn't become normal."

### Exercise 2.3: Upside, downside, my side
**Time:** 7 min · **Type:** record

For each topic, answer in **three sentences**: the upside ("The upside is…"), the downside ("The downside is…" / "The catch is…"), and your side ("For me, … wins, because…"). Aim for 30 seconds each. Record topics 1–4, listen to one, then do 5–8.

1. Using AI to write first drafts at work
2. Smart speakers in the home
3. Working with people on the other side of the world over video calls
4. Free apps that show you adverts
5. Kids learning to code at primary school
6. Face recognition to unlock your phone
7. Getting all your news from social media
8. Wearing a fitness tracker every day

### Answer key: Day 2

1. "It's basically a way of keeping your files on the internet instead of on your computer. So, like, if you lose your phone, your photos are still safe."
2. "It's basically when you show two versions of something to different people and see which one works better. So, like, half the people see a green button, half see a blue one."
3. "It's basically a shared kit of buttons, colours and rules, so everything in a product looks and works the same. So, like, a LEGO set for an app."
4. "It's basically a program that answers simple questions automatically, like a customer service person that never sleeps."
5. "It's basically when you need two things to log in, your password plus a code on your phone. So, like, a second lock on the door."
6. "It's basically the system that decides which posts you see first, based on what you looked at before."
7. "It's basically a fake version of an app that you can click through, so you can test the idea before anyone builds it."
8. "It's basically when a computer translates text for you automatically. Like when you point your phone at a menu."
9. "It's basically a heating control that learns when you're home and sets the temperature for you."
10. "It's basically when they fix small mistakes in the app that made things break or act weird."

**2.2 Model answers (prediction 2)**

- 50%: "It could go either way whether every design tool has AI built in."
- 30%: "I doubt every design tool will have AI built in."

b. "It's made my work way easier and faster."
c. "I could see AI changing a lot of design work in the next five years." (or "I doubt AI will replace all designers in five years.")
d. "I'm not convinced it'll happen."
e. "I'd be surprised if it didn't become normal." (after "I'd be surprised if", use the past: "didn't")

**2.3 Model answers**
1. "The upside is it saves a ton of time, especially on boring stuff. The downside is, if you're not careful, everything sounds the same. For me, the upside wins, because I still edit everything myself."
4. "The upside is you get the app for free. The catch is, you're kind of paying with your attention and your data. For me, it depends on the app, but for most stuff I'd rather pay a little and skip the ads."

**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

These sentences are too harsh or too general. Rewrite each one out loud so it's fair. Use at least one softener ("In general…", "A lot of people…", "It's not everyone, but…", "At least in my experience…", "In the city where I grew up…") and describe instead of judging.

2. "People back home are always late."
3. "Everyone in the US eats huge portions."
4. "People back home don't care about rules."
5. "Americans only talk about work."
6. "Nobody back home says what they really think."
7. "Americans don't know anything about other countries."
8. "Old people back home never use technology."
9. "Everyone in the US drives everywhere."
10. "People back home are too obsessed with family."

### Exercise 3.2: Explain the custom in four steps
**Time:** 8 min · **Type:** record

Explain each custom in **four steps: what, when, why, feel**. Use "It's a… thing", "The idea behind it is…" and one "whereas here…". About 1 minute each.

**Part A, from back home** (use a real custom from where you're from for each):
1. How people greet older relatives
2. What guests bring when they visit someone's home
3. Who pays when friends go out to eat
4. A special food for a holiday or celebration
5. How people say no politely

**Part B, American customs** (explain them as if to a friend back home who's never been to the US):
6. Tipping in restaurants
7. "How are you?" when it isn't a real question
8. A potluck dinner
9. Small talk with the cashier in a store

### Exercise 3.3: Stereotype comebacks
**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

Someone says each comment below. They're not being mean, just a bit lazy. Respond in **three moves**: (1) correct it lightly ("Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually…"), (2) give a real detail that shows a fuller picture, (3) ask a question back. Say each response twice. Comments 1–4 are about back home; 5–6 are about Americans.

1. "Oh, so people there must eat really spicy food all the time, right?"
2. "Isn't it, like, really dangerous over there?"
3. "I bet everyone there is super strict and serious."
4. "So you probably all live with, like, ten family members?"
5. A friend from back home says: "Americans are all loud and rude, right?"
6. A friend from back home says: "In the US everyone's rich, aren't they?"

### Answer key: Day 3

**3.1 Model answers**
1. "At first, the friendliness felt a little surface-level to me. But I think it's more that, in general, being friendly with strangers is just polite here."
2. "In the city where I grew up, a lot of people are pretty relaxed about time, at least for social stuff. Work is different."
3. "Portions here are often way bigger than back home, at least in restaurants. It took me a while to get used to it."
4. "It's not everyone, but a lot of people back home are more flexible about small rules, like where to park."
5. "At least in my experience, people here talk about work a lot more when they first meet you. Back home, you'd ask about family first."
6. "In general, people back home are more indirect. They'd rather hint than say no straight out."
7. "A lot of people I've met here haven't travelled much, which makes sense, the country's so big. But some of them know way more than I expected."
8. "It's not everyone, but a lot of older people back home aren't super into technology. My grandma's on video calls every day, though."
9. "In a lot of US cities, people drive almost everywhere, whereas back home we walk or take the bus more."
10. "Family's a really big deal back home, in general. Like, Sunday is basically family day. It's not better or worse, just a different priority."

**3.2 Model answer (6. Tipping)**
"So in the US, when you eat at a restaurant, you add a tip on top of the bill, usually something like fifteen to twenty percent. It happens pretty much anywhere with table service, and a lot of cafés and bars too. The idea behind it is that a lot of the server's pay comes from tips, so it's not really optional. It's a 'this is how they get paid' thing. Back home we don't really tip, or just round up, whereas here, not tipping is kind of rude. Honestly, it took me a while to get used to it. I kept doing the maths at the table, like, in panic."

**3.2 Model answer (7. "How are you?")**
"So here, people say 'How are you?' all the time, like a cashier, or someone you pass in the office. It's basically just 'hello'. When does it happen? Pretty much any time you see someone. The idea behind it is just to be friendly, not to actually ask about your life. So you just say, 'Good, thanks! You?' and keep walking. What threw me at first was that I'd start actually answering, like, 'Well, I'm kinda tired, because…', and they'd already be gone. Back home, if someone asks how you are, they mean it."

**3.3 Model answers**
1. "Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually. Some dishes are spicy, sure, but my mom's cooking is super mild. Like, the most popular stuff is rice and stews. What's the food back home for you?"
2. "Ha, I get why people think that, but that's kind of a stereotype. Where I grew up, it's honestly pretty normal: people going to work, kids at the park. Like anywhere, there are some areas you'd avoid. Have you ever travelled somewhere that surprised you like that?"
3. "Ha, not really! Some people, sure, but my friends back home are actually hilarious. The humour's just different, a bit drier. What's American humour like, in your opinion?"
4. "Ha, ten's a lot! It's not everyone, but yeah, a lot of people live with their parents longer, until they get married sometimes. It's more of a money and family thing. Is that weird here?"
5. "Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually. Some people are louder, sure, but most people I've met are super polite, like, they say sorry when *you* bump into *them*. It's just a different style. Have you ever met an American?"
6. "Ha, no way. Some people are, but rent and healthcare are really expensive here, so a lot of people actually struggle. It's not what it looks like on TV. What made you think that, the films?"

3. Is university worth it?

### Exercise 5.2: Make the other side strong
**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

Each sentence describes the other side unfairly. Rewrite it out loud so it's the **strongest, fairest version** of that side. Start with "To be fair to the other side…", "The best argument for it is…" or "I can see why people would…".

4. "Anyone who still pays with cash is stuck in the past."

### Exercise 5.3: Five-part answers against the clock

### Answer key: Day 5

**5.1 Model answers**

6. "It depends on the trip. If it's a city full of people, then alone is great, you meet people; if it's somewhere remote, then go with friends, it's safer and more fun. So for me, I'd try alone at least once."

**5.2 Model answers**

4. "To be fair to the other side, cash is private, it works when the internet's down, and some people budget better when they can see the money."
5. "To be fair to the other side, a lot of experts are worried too. The concern is about things like jobs, fairness and mistakes, not about not understanding the tech."
6. "I can see why people would rent. It's flexible, you're not stuck with repairs, and in some cities buying just doesn't make financial sense."

**5.3 Model answer (Motion 6)**
"OK, it's a tricky one, because it's really about fairness versus privacy. The best argument for it is fairness: if salaries are public, it's way harder to pay two people differently for the same job, and people can actually negotiate. To be fair to the other side, though, a lot of people feel their pay is private, and it could create tension in a team, like, 'why does she earn more than me?' Where it gets tricky is that pay isn't always simple: experience, performance, when you joined. So on balance, where I land is: publish the salary *ranges* for each role, not everyone's exact number. That gets you most of the fairness without the drama. Where would you land?"
