## Learning objectives

- Generalise about a culture carefully, so you sound fair and not like a stereotype.
- Explain a custom from back home to someone who's never seen it: what it is, when it happens, why, and how it feels.
- Describe a culture surprise ("what threw me at first was…") and how you got used to it.
- Ask good questions about other people's cultures, and respond calmly when someone stereotypes yours.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Feature: comparison signposts in long sentences.** When people compare two places in a podcast, the sentence has two halves: "back home, people…, | whereas here, people…". The trick is the **balance**:

1. The first half **rises** at the end, because it's waiting for the second half: "Back HOME, dinner's at like NINE ↗, |"
2. The comparison word (**whereas**, **but here**, **while in the States**) gets a small stress, then the second half **falls** at the end: "whereas HERE it's more like SIX ↘."

Practise. CAPITALS = stress, ↗ = rising, ↘ = falling, | = tiny pause.

1. Back HOME, dinner's at like NINE ↗, | whereas HERE it's more like SIX ↘. ("whereas" → "wair-AZ")
2. Where I'm FROM, you don't really TIP ↗, | but in the STATES it's kind of EXPECTED ↘.
3. In GENeral, people are more DIRECT here ↗, | at least at WORK ↘.
4. It's not EVERYbody, obviously, | but a LOT of people back home live with their PARENTS till they get MARRIED.
5. What THREW me at first | was how FRIENDLY strangers are in STORES. ("threw me" → "THROO-mee")
6. Here, "let's get COFFEE sometime" ↗ | doesn't always MEAN you're getting coffee ↘.
7. Back HOME, if someone offers you FOOD, ↗ | you're supposed to say NO a couple TIMES first ↘.
8. It's kinda the OPPOSITE, actually: | here you're LATE if you're on TIME. ("kinda the" → "kinda-thuh")
9. I USED to think it was RUDE, | but now I get it: it's just a different STYLE.
10. It's not BETTER or WORSE, | it's just DIFFERENT.

**Clip to find:** people talking about moving to the US or living between two cultures. Search: "culture shock America vlog", "things that surprised me about the US podcast", "living abroad conversation podcast", "expat friends discuss cultural differences". Choose a clip where the speaker chats, not a top-ten list read from a script.

**Method:** speak along with 2 minutes, three times ([Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md)). Pause-and-repeat the two-half comparison sentences until the rise in the first half feels natural.

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Culture and differences

### Explanation

Americans love asking "So what's it like where you're from?" It's a friendly question, and it's a chance for you to talk for a while about something you know really well. But culture talk has a trap: **generalising**. If you say "People in my country are lazy" or "Americans are fake", you sound unfair, and you might offend someone.

Native speakers handle this with three habits:

1. **Soften the generalisation.** "In general…", "A lot of people…", "It's not everyone, but…", "At least in my experience…", "In the city where I grew up…". These small phrases make the same point but leave room for exceptions.
2. **Describe, don't judge.** Say what people *do*, not whether it's good. "People eat dinner really late" is a description. "People eat dinner at a stupid time" is a judgement. If you do judge, make it personal and light: "I'll be honest, I miss the late dinners."
3. **Explain the "why".** A custom only makes sense when you know the reason behind it. "You say no to food a couple of times first, because accepting straight away can look a bit greedy. It's about being polite."

**Explaining a custom in four steps:**

| Step | Question it answers | Example |
|---|---|---|
| What | What is it? | "So back home, when someone offers you food, you're supposed to say no a couple of times first." |
| When | When does it happen? | "Mostly when you visit someone's house, especially older people." |
| Why | Why do people do it? | "It's a politeness thing. Accepting right away can seem a bit greedy." |
| Feel | What's it like, or how does it compare? | "So when I first came here, I said no once and the host just… didn't offer again. I was starving!" |

**When someone stereotypes your culture**, stay relaxed. Correct it gently and add a real detail: "Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually. Some people, sure, but…". Then ask them something back. Calm and curious always wins.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Where I'm from… / Back home… | You introduce your culture without naming it every time | "Back home, Sunday is basically family day." |
| In general… / Generally speaking… | You make a careful generalisation | "In general, people are a bit more formal at work." |
| It's not everyone, but… | You stop a generalisation from becoming a stereotype | "It's not everyone, but a lot of people live with their parents for longer." |
| At least in my experience… | You make it personal and limited | "At least in my experience, people here are super friendly in stores." |
| …whereas here… | You compare two places in one sentence | "Back home we take long lunches, whereas here people eat at their desks." |
| It's kind of the opposite. | The difference is very big | "With being on time, it's kind of the opposite." |
| What threw me at first was… | You describe a culture surprise | "What threw me at first was how much people talk to strangers." |
| It took me a while to get used to… | You talk about adapting | "It took me a while to get used to tipping everywhere." |
| It's a… thing. | You name the type of custom simply | "It's a respect thing. You stand up when an older person comes in." |
| The idea behind it is… | You explain the "why" | "The idea behind it is that the guest should never feel like a burden." |
| It's not better or worse, it's just different. | You avoid judging | "Honestly, it's not better or worse, it's just a different style." |
| That's kind of a stereotype, actually. | Someone generalises about your culture | "Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually. Some people, sure." |
| Is that a thing here? / Is that normal here? | You ask about American customs | "Bringing a gift to a dinner party, is that a thing here?" |
| What's the equivalent where you're from? | You turn it into a two-way conversation | "We have a big family dinner at New Year. What's the equivalent where you're from?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: a coworker asks about back home**

> **A:** So what's work culture like where you're from? Is it super different?
> **B:** In some ways, yeah. In general, it's a bit more formal. Like, you wouldn't call your boss by their first name, at least not in a big company. It's not everyone, but a lot of places are pretty hierarchical.
> **A:** Interesting.
> **B:** And what threw me at first here was how direct people are in meetings. Like, someone just says, "I don't think that'll work," in front of everyone. Back home you'd say that privately, after the meeting. The idea behind it is you don't want anyone to lose face. So I used to think people here were kinda rude, but now I get it. It's not better or worse, it's just a different style. Is it like that everywhere in the US, or is it a tech thing?

**Example 2: explaining a custom**

> **A:** Wait, so you said no to the cake three times? Why?
> **B:** [laughs] It's a politeness thing. Back home, when someone offers you food, you're supposed to say no a couple of times first. Mostly at someone's house, especially with older people. The idea behind it is you don't wanna look greedy, and the host is supposed to insist.
> **A:** Oh my god, and I just took the cake away.
> **B:** Exactly! It took me a while to get used to it here. Now I just say yes the first time. What's the equivalent where you're from? Is there a "polite" way to say yes?

**Example 3: handling a stereotype calmly**

> **A:** Oh, so everyone there is super religious, right?
> **B:** Ha, that's kind of a stereotype, actually. Some people, sure, same as anywhere. But in the city where I grew up, it's really mixed. My friends were all kinds of different. At least in my experience, people care more about family than anything else.
> **A:** Oh, OK, fair.
> **B:** It's like if I said everyone in America eats fast food every day. [laughs] Is that a thing here, though? Family Sunday dinners?

### Common mistakes

❌ "Americans are all fake. They smile but they don't mean it."
✅ "At first I felt like the friendliness wasn't always real. But I think it's more that here, being friendly is just polite, like a default setting."
*A harsh generalisation sounds unfair. Describe your experience and explain the difference.*

❌ "In my country, we are eating dinner at nine."
✅ "Back home, we usually eat dinner at like nine."
*Habits and customs use the present simple, often with "usually". The continuous is for right now.*

❌ "The people there is very friendly."
✅ "People there are really friendly."
*"People" is plural: "people are". And you usually don't need "the" for people in general.*

❌ "It's the culture." (as the whole explanation)
✅ "It's a respect thing. The idea behind it is…"
*"It's the culture" explains nothing. Give the actual reason.*

❌ Talking for five minutes about your country and never asking anything.
✅ "…What's the equivalent where you're from?"
*Culture talk should go both ways. End with a question, every time.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

### Part A: Random-topic warm-up (15 min)

Use [the random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md), but turn every topic into a comparison. Talk for 1 minute each, starting with "Back home…" and using "whereas here…" at least once. No preparation.

1. Breakfast
2. Weddings
3. Being late
4. Birthdays
5. Public transport
6. Grandparents
7. Office lunches
8. Saying thank you
9. Holidays and days off
10. Talking to strangers

Do at least six. Record them all.

### Part B: 3/2/1 culture comparison (25 min)

Use [the 3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md). Prompt: **"What's one thing about life back home that would surprise an American, and one thing about American life that surprised you (or would surprise you)?"** If you've never lived in the US, use what you've noticed from American colleagues, films and shows.

Your answer must include:
- one softened generalisation ("It's not everyone, but…")
- one custom explained in four steps (what, when, why, feel)
- one "whereas here…" comparison
- one "it's not better or worse, it's just different" moment
- a question at the end

3 minutes, then 2, then 1, recording each round.

### Part C: Culture role-plays (25 min)

Play both roles out loud. Eight minutes each, then listen back to the first two minutes of one of them.

- **Role-play 1: The curious new colleague.**
  - *Jordan,* an American designer who joined your team last week, asks you over lunch: "So what's the work culture like where you're from? What would surprise me?"
  - *Your mission:* give two real differences, soften every generalisation, explain the "why" of each, and ask Jordan two questions back about American work culture.
- **Role-play 2: The dinner guest.**
  - *You* are hosting an American friend, Taylor, for dinner, cooking food from back home. Taylor keeps asking: "What's this? Do people eat this every day? Is there a right way to eat it?"
  - *Your mission:* explain one dish and one table custom in four steps, keep it light and funny, and use "It's a… thing" at least once.
- **Role-play 3: The stereotype.**
  - *Chris,* someone you just met at a meetup, says something like "Oh, people from there are really serious, right? Nobody jokes?" He means no harm.
  - *Your mission:* correct it calmly ("That's kind of a stereotype, actually…"), give a real example that shows a different picture, make the comparison with an American stereotype light and friendly, and move the conversation forward with a question.

### Part D: Listen back (5 min)

Listen to your Part B round 3. Count your [3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md) and log them in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md). Did you soften every generalisation? Note any that slipped through.
