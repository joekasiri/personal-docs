## Learning objectives

- Bring up a news-type story naturally in conversation, without sounding like a newsreader.
- Summarise a story in four steps: what happened, the background, why it matters, what happens next.
- Say where you heard something and how sure you are ("apparently", "from what I read", "I might be getting this wrong, but…").
- Keep the facts separate from your opinion, and hand the topic to the other person.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**This week's feature: long sentences and signposting.** In discussion podcasts, people often speak in long sentences. They don't get lost because they use **signposts**: short phrases that tell the listener what's coming ("So the background is…", "The interesting part is…"). Tonight's signposts are the ones that introduce **background and context**.

Listen for two things:
1. The signpost is said a little **faster and lower**, almost like a quick "here we go". Then the speaker **pauses for a tiny moment** before the important part.
2. In a long sentence, the voice **doesn't fall to the bottom** until the very end. In the middle, it stays up, which tells the listener "I'm not finished".

Practise these sentences. Read each one once slowly, then at full speed. CAPITALS = stressed words, | = tiny pause.

1. So the BACKground is | the city's been talking about this for YEARS.
2. Basically what HAPPENED was | they tested it in ONE neighbourhood first.
3. The thing you have to underSTAND is | it's not a NEW idea.
4. For CONtext, | this has been going on since, like, LAST year. ("since, like, LAST year" → "sinsl-like-LAST-year")
5. From what I READ, | it's mostly about COST, not SAFETY.
6. Apparently they tried it in a few OTHER places, | and it kinda WORKED. ("kinda WORKED" → "kinda-WERKT")
7. The BIG picture is | people are SPENDING less, | and SAVING more.
8. What made it a big DEAL | was that NObody saw it COMING.
9. I'm not a hundred percent SURE, | but I THINK it starts next MONTH. ("a hundred percent" → "a HUN-dred per-SENT")
10. So, long story SHORT, | they're gonna VOTE on it in the SPRING. ("gonna" → "gunna")

**Clip to find:** two or three hosts discussing one topic, where one host explains the background to the others. Search: "two hosts discuss podcast", "explained podcast conversation", "news discussion podcast hosts", "deep dive podcast episode". Avoid clips that are read from a script. You want people who say "so basically" and "wait, so…".

**Method:** speak along with a 2-minute stretch, three times, with no pausing ([Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md)). Then pick the two longest sentences and do pause-and-repeat on them until you can say each one in a single breath group.

## 2. Main skill (20 min): News and current events

### Explanation

When people talk about the news casually, they don't present it like a TV reporter. They **bring it up**, **summarise it quickly**, **say how they know**, and then **turn it into a conversation**. The hard part for learners is not the vocabulary. It's that they try to retell *everything*, in order, and get lost halfway.

Use this simple shape. It works for any story: a new law, a company announcement, a scientific study, something in your city.

| Step | What you do | Sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Hook | Bring it up and give the headline in one sentence | "Did you see that thing about the four-day week?" |
| 2. Background | One or two sentences of context | "So basically, a bunch of companies tried it for six months…" |
| 3. Why it matters | Why anyone should care | "And the interesting part is, nobody got less work done." |
| 4. What's next | What happens now, or what's still unknown | "Now they're deciding whether to keep it." |
| 5. Hand over | Your quick reaction + a question | "I think it's kinda cool, honestly. Would you want that?" |

Two rules make you sound natural and fair:

- **Say where it came from and how sure you are.** You don't need exact sources, but you shouldn't sound more certain than you are. "Apparently", "From what I read", "I saw a headline, I didn't read the whole thing" are all normal and honest. ([Week 11 Day 3](../../month-03-spontaneity/week-11-never-stopping/day-3-talking-when-youre-unsure.md) taught the basic "I'm not sure, but…". Tonight is specifically about *reporting things you heard*.)
- **Separate the fact from your opinion.** First say what happened. Then say "Personally…" or "My take is…". When people mix them, conversations about the news turn into arguments fast.

A note on topics: with people you don't know well, stories about science, cities, work, technology, health studies, sport and culture are safe. Politics and religion can be fine with friends, but read the room. A good escape line is "I don't know enough about it to have a strong opinion, to be honest."

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Did you see that thing about…? | You bring up a story casually | "Did you see that thing about the city banning cars downtown?" |
| I was reading something the other day about… | You bring it up more slowly, less like breaking news | "I was reading something the other day about people moving out of big cities." |
| So basically what happened was… | You start the summary | "So basically what happened was, they tested a four-day week at a bunch of companies." |
| For context… / The background is… | You add background | "For context, they've been talking about this for, like, two years." |
| Apparently… | You report something you heard but didn't check | "Apparently it worked better than anyone expected." |
| From what I read… / From what I understand… | You show your info might be incomplete | "From what I understand, it only applies to new buildings." |
| I only saw the headline, so… | You're honest that you know very little | "I only saw the headline, so I might be wrong about the details." |
| The interesting part is… / What's interesting is… | You say why it matters | "The interesting part is that the people who complained most ended up liking it." |
| It's still kind of up in the air. | Nothing is decided yet | "Whether they'll keep it is still kind of up in the air." |
| There's a lot of back and forth about… | People disagree and it's ongoing | "There's a lot of back and forth about whether it's worth the money." |
| Personally… / My take is… | You switch from facts to your opinion | "My take is, it's a good idea, but it won't work for every job." |
| I don't know enough about it to have a strong opinion. | You want to stay neutral or you really don't know | "Honestly, I don't know enough about it to have a strong opinion." |
| Have you been following it? / Have you heard about it? | You hand the topic over | "Have you been following it at all?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: at lunch with a colleague**

> **A:** Did you see that thing about the four-day week trial?
> **B:** No, what happened?
> **A:** So basically, a bunch of companies tried it for six months. Same pay, one day less. For context, they'd been talking about it for ages, but nobody had actually tested it properly.
> **B:** Huh. And?
> **A:** Apparently most of them are keeping it. The interesting part is, productivity didn't really drop. People were just, like, more focused on the days they were in.
> **B:** No way. I'd be so into that.
> **A:** Right? From what I read, it worked way better for office jobs than for, like, hospitals or stores. So it's still kind of up in the air for a lot of industries. My take is, it's a great idea, but it's not one-size-fits-all. Would you want that, though? Or would you rather have shorter days?

**Example 2: being honest that you only half know the story**

> **A:** Have you heard about the new rule for delivery robots?
> **B:** Kind of? I only saw the headline, so I might be getting this wrong. But I think they're letting them use bike lanes now?
> **A:** Yeah, something like that.
> **B:** From what I understand, it's just a test in a couple of neighborhoods. There's a lot of back and forth about safety. Personally, I don't know enough about it to have a strong opinion yet. I'd wanna see how it goes. Have you seen one of them?

**Example 3: separating the facts from the opinion**

> **A:** So I was reading something the other day about this study on remote work and loneliness.
> **B:** Oh yeah?
> **A:** Yeah. The background is, they followed, like, a few thousand people for a year. What they found was that fully remote people felt lonelier, but people who came in two or three days a week were actually the happiest group.
> **B:** Interesting.
> **A:** That's what the study says, anyway. My take is, it depends a lot on your team. Like, on my team, the office days are kind of the fun days. But I can totally see it being the opposite somewhere else. What's it like at your place?

### Common mistakes

❌ "I want to tell you a news. A news said that…"
✅ "I saw something in the news…" / "There was this story about…"
*"News" is uncountable. You can't say "a news". Say "a story", "a piece of news" or "something in the news".*

❌ "The news say that prices are going up."
✅ "The news says…" or, more naturally, "Apparently prices are going up."
*"News" takes a singular verb, but in casual talk "apparently" is easier and more natural.*

❌ Starting from the very beginning: "So, three years ago, the government had a meeting, and then in the meeting they…"
✅ Headline first: "So they're making public transport free for students. For context, they've been talking about it for years."
*Listeners need the point first. Background comes second, in one or two sentences.*

❌ "It is sure that this will fail." (said about something you only read a headline about)
✅ "From what I read, it sounds like it might not work. But I only saw the headline."
*Sounding more certain than you are makes people push back hard. Match your confidence to what you actually know.*

❌ "And what do you think about it? Do you agree with it? Is it good or bad?"
✅ "Would you want that where you work?"
*One clear, personal question is easier to answer than three general ones.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

Tonight is a mixed drill. Every story in it is invented, so you can practise the *language* of news without worrying about the facts.

**Part A: Random-topic warm-up (15 min)**
Use [the random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md). Pick five cards from the harder section of [the random topic cards](../../toolkit/random-topics.md). For each one, talk for 1 minute, but begin as if it's a news story: "So I was reading something the other day about [topic]…". Invent the "story" as you go. No preparation, no stopping. Record all five.

**Part B: 3/2/1 news summary (25 min)**
Use [the 3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md). Choose **one** of these invented stories and tell it using the five steps (hook, background, why it matters, what's next, hand over). Tell it in 3 minutes, then 2, then 1, recording each round.

- **Story 1:** A mid-sized city has decided to close its main shopping street to cars every weekend for a year. Shop owners were worried at first, but early numbers show more people walking in. Delivery companies are unhappy. The city will review it in the autumn.
- **Story 2:** A large company announced that it's dropping job titles for everyone below director level. Instead, people will just have a team name. Some employees love it, others say it makes promotions confusing. Other companies are watching to see what happens.
- **Story 3:** A university study found that people who take a 20-minute walk after lunch make fewer mistakes in afternoon work. The study was small, about 200 people, and some researchers say it needs to be repeated before anyone takes it seriously.

In round 3 (1 minute), you only have time for the headline, one line of background, one line of "why it matters", and your question. That's the point.

**Part C: "Dinner table" role-plays (25 min)**
Play both roles out loud, switching your voice or position for each person. Do each role-play for 8 minutes, then listen back to the first 2 minutes.

- **Role-play 1: Lunch with a colleague.**
  - *You:* You read about Story 1 (the car-free shopping street) and you think it's a good idea.
  - *Your colleague, Dana:* drives to work, lives in the suburbs, and is worried about parking. She's not angry, just doubtful.
  - *Your mission:* summarise the story fairly, keep facts and opinion separate, and ask Dana at least two real questions about her view.
- **Role-play 2: A friend who's heard it wrong.**
  - *You:* You read Story 2 (no job titles) properly.
  - *Your friend, Marco:* saw only the headline and thinks the company is firing all its managers.
  - *Your mission:* correct him politely ("From what I read, it's actually more like…"), explain what's still up in the air, and say what you personally think.
- **Role-play 3: You know almost nothing.**
  - *Your manager, Priya,* brings up Story 3 (the walking study) and asks what you think.
  - *You:* You only saw the headline.
  - *Your mission:* be honest ("I only saw the headline, so…"), say what you *do* know, ask her a question to learn more, and give a careful reaction.

**Part D: Listen back (5 min)**
Listen to your Part B round 3. Count your [3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md) and write them in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md). Note one place where you said too much background.
