- In [Week 17 Day 3: Pushing back politely](../week-17-meetings/day-3-pushing-back-politely.md) you learned to say "no" to *someone else's* request or idea. Tonight is the other side: **your** idea is under attack, and you want to keep it alive.

- Think of one **real decision or proposal** from your project that someone questioned, or could question. You'll defend it tonight.

## Learning objectives

- Defend an idea in four calm steps: **acknowledge → reason → evidence → offer**.
- Stay friendly and confident when someone challenges you more than once.
- Turn an objection into a **test or compromise** instead of a fight.
- Know when to **give ground** gracefully, and say it in a way that still sounds strong.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Focus: confident openers with falling intonation.** When people defend an idea well, their first words go **down** at the end, like a full stop. Rising intonation ("I think we should keep it?") sounds like you're asking permission. Falling intonation ("I think we should keep it.") sounds like a decision. Tonight, exaggerate the fall: let your voice drop on the last stressed word, and don't rush the sentence.

| # | Sentence | How it sounds (↘ = voice falls) |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | I hear you, and I'd still go with it. ↘ | "I HEAR you, 'n I'd STILL go with it ↘" |
| 2 | That's a fair point. ↘ Here's why I'm sticking with it. ↘ | "that's a FAIR point ↘. here's WHY I'm STICKing with it ↘" |
| 3 | The data's pretty clear on this one. ↘ | "the DAY-da's pretty CLEAR on this one ↘" |
| 4 | I don't think that's a deal-breaker. ↘ | "I don't think that's a DEAL-breaker ↘" |
| 5 | Let's not throw out the whole thing because of one risk. ↘ | "let's NOT throw out the WHOLE thing b'cuz of ONE risk ↘" |
| 6 | What would it take to convince you? ↘ | "WHAT would it take ta con-VINCE you ↘" |
| 7 | OK, you've got me there. ↘ | "o-KAY, you've GOT me there ↘" |

**Clip to find:** a debate-style podcast or a startup pitch with tough judges, where someone defends their position calmly. ## 2. Main skill (20 min): Defending an idea

### Explanation

When someone attacks your idea in a meeting, there are two bad reactions: **cave in** ("Oh, OK, maybe you're right, forget it") or **fight** ("No, you don't understand"). Both happen because you feel pressure and grab the first thing. A good defence has four steps, and you can practise them until they're automatic:

1. **Acknowledge.** Show you heard the concern, and that it's reasonable. "That's a fair point." This lowers the temperature. It is *not* agreeing.
2. **Reason.** Restate the *one* main reason for your idea in a single sentence. Not three reasons. One. "The whole point is to get people to their first project faster."
3. **Evidence.** Give one fact, test result, example or user quote. "In testing, seven out of eight people finished without help."
4. **Offer.** Make it easy for the other person to say yes: a test, a time limit, a compromise, or a way to measure it. "Let's run it for two weeks and look at the numbers."

If they push again, **don't repeat louder**. Go to a question: "What would it take to convince you?" or "What's the part that worries you most?" Now they have to be specific, and you can deal with the specific thing.

**Giving ground is part of defending.** If they make a point you can't answer, admit it quickly and confidently: "OK, you've got me there. That part needs work." Then protect what's still strong: "But I don't think that means we drop the whole idea." People trust you more when you can concede small things.

**Language tip: use "I" and "we", not "you".** "You're wrong about the data" is an attack. "I'm reading the data differently" is a discussion.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| That's a fair point, and… | you acknowledge without giving in | "That's a fair point, and it's something we thought about a lot." |
| I hear you, and I'd still go with it. | you've listened, but you're keeping your position | "I hear you, and I'd still go with it. Here's why." |
| The whole point of this is… | you restate your main reason | "The whole point of this is to cut the steps from five to two." |
| That's actually why we… | you turn their objection into your argument | "That's actually why we kept the old menu as a fallback." |
| Here's what we saw in testing. | you bring evidence | "Here's what we saw in testing: nobody looked for the old button." |
| I'm reading the data a bit differently. | you disagree about the facts, calmly | "I'm reading the data a bit differently. The drop was only on desktop." |
| I don't think that's a deal-breaker. | the risk is real but small | "Some users will be confused at first, but I don't think that's a deal-breaker." |
| Let's not throw the baby out with the bathwater. | they want to drop everything because of one problem | "The colours need work, sure. But let's not throw the baby out with the bathwater." |
| What would it take to convince you? | they keep pushing and you want specifics | "OK, what would it take to convince you? A test? More interviews?" |
| What's the part that worries you most? | the objection is vague | "What's the part that worries you most: the timeline or the users?" |
| How about we test it with… and see? | you offer a low-risk way forward | "How about we test it with ten percent of users and see?" |
| If it doesn't work, we roll it back. | you lower the risk for them | "Give it two weeks. If it doesn't work, we roll it back." |
| OK, you've got me there. | they made a point you can't answer | "OK, you've got me there. I hadn't thought about the legal side." |
| I'll give you that, but… | you concede a small point and keep the big one | "I'll give you that, it's more work for engineering. But it saves support a ton of time." |

### Examples

**Example 1: Defending a design decision in a review**

> **Kevin (PM):** I'm not sure about removing the onboarding tutorial. Won't new users be totally lost?
> **You:** That's a fair point, and it was our biggest worry too. The whole point of removing it is to get people into the product faster. Right now, sixty percent of people skip the tutorial anyway. And in testing, seven out of eight people created their first project without any help.
> **Kevin:** Seven out of eight people in a lab isn't the real world, though.
> **You:** I'll give you that. So how about we test it with ten percent of new sign-ups for two weeks? If activation goes down, we roll it back.
> **Kevin:** OK, that I can live with.

**Example 2: The second push, and asking for specifics**

> **Grace (engineering manager):** I still think it's too much work for this quarter.
> **You:** I hear you. What's the part that worries you most: the new components or the testing?
> **Grace:** The components. We'd need to build four new ones.
> **You:** That's actually why we designed it with only two new ones and two from the design system. So it's really two, not four. Would that change things?
> **Grace:** Hmm. If it's two, maybe.

**Example 3: Giving ground gracefully**

> **Omar (legal):** You can't save people's location by default. That's a privacy issue in some countries.
> **You:** OK, you've got me there. I hadn't thought about the legal side at all. So let's make location off by default. But I don't think that means we drop the whole feature. People who turn it on still get the faster check-in.
> **Omar:** That works.

### Common mistakes

❌ "Yes, but you don't understand the user research."
✅ "That's a fair point. Here's what we saw in the research…"
*"You don't understand" makes it personal. Acknowledge, then bring evidence.*

❌ Giving five reasons in a row when challenged.
✅ "The whole point of this is to get people to their first project faster." (one reason) + one piece of evidence.
*One strong reason is more convincing than five weak ones, and much easier to say under pressure.*

❌ Repeating your argument louder when they push again.
✅ "What would it take to convince you?"
*A question moves the conversation forward. Repeating doesn't.*

❌ "OK… sorry… you are right, let's forget it." *(after one objection)*
✅ "I'll give you that part. But I'd still keep the rest."
*Concede the small point, defend the big one.*

❌ "I think we should keep it?" *(rising voice)*
✅ "I think we should keep it." *(falling voice)*
*Rising intonation sounds like a question. Let your voice fall.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

**Step 1: Warm-up random topic (10 min).** Use the [random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md) with 4 cards from the "hard" group of [random topics](../../toolkit/random-topics.md), or these opinion topics, where you take a side and defend it for 2 minutes each: *remote work is better than the office · meetings should never be longer than 30 minutes · every designer should learn to code · good design is invisible*.

**Step 2: 3/2/1 defence (20 min).** Use the [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md). Prompt: **"Your team wants to cancel your project. Defend it: why it matters, what the evidence says, and what you'd offer to keep it going."** Use your own project from this week. Do 3 minutes, 2 minutes, 1 minute. In every version, include all four steps: acknowledge, reason, evidence, offer.

**Step 3: Role-play, one idea vs three challengers (30 min).** Use the role cards in [Exercise 3.4](exercises.md#exercise-34-three-challengers-role-cards-drill-material). The idea you're defending: **"We should launch the redesigned app to all users next month, not in a slow rollout."** For each challenger:

1. Read the challenger's lines out loud in their voice (record yourself playing both sides).
2. Respond with the four-step defence.
3. When the challenger pushes a second time, use a question ("What would it take to convince you?").
4. With at least one challenger, **give ground** on one point.

Do all three challengers (about 7 minutes each). Then do a final 3-minute round where all three challengers' objections come one after another, and you answer each in 30 seconds.

**Step 4: Listen back (10 min).** Listen to the role-play. Count your [3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md) in a 2-minute section. Then check: did your voice fall at the end of your defending sentences, or did it rise? Find one rising sentence and say it again with a fall.
