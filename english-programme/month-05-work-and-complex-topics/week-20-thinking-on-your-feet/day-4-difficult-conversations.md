## Before you start

- Use a quiet room. Hard conversations feel more real when you stand up and speak at full volume.

## Learning objectives

- Deliver bad news in a clear order: **headline → ownership → short reason → options → next step**.
- Stay calm and low-voiced when the other person is frustrated or angry.
- Say "no" to a boss or client and offer what you *can* do instead.
- Use empathy phrases that sound real, not like a script.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Focus: calm, low openers under pressure.** When a good interviewee gets an aggressive question, their voice often goes **lower and slower**, not higher and faster. That signals calm. A high, fast voice signals panic, even with perfect grammar. Tonight, speak each sentence about 20% slower than feels normal, keep your pitch low, and stress only one or two words.

| # | Sentence | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | I understand. Let me explain what happened. | "I under-STAND. lemme ex-PLAIN what HAPPened" (low, slow) |
| 2 | I'll be straight with you. We're gonna miss Friday. | "I'll be STRAIGHT with you. we're gonna MISS FRI-day" |
| 3 | That's on me. I should've flagged it sooner. | "that's ON me. I should've FLAGGED it SOON-er" |
| 4 | I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair. | "I can HEAR that you're FRUS-trated, 'n that's FAIR" |
| 5 | Here's what we CAN do. | "HERE'S what we CAN do" |
| 6 | I'm not able to promise that, but I can promise this. | "I'm NOT able ta PROM-ise that, but I CAN promise THIS" |
| 7 | Let's figure out the best option together. | "let's FIG-yer out the BEST option to-GETH-er" |

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Difficult conversations

### Explanation

Difficult conversations at work usually fall into three groups:

- **Bad news upwards:** telling your boss something went wrong (a deadline will slip, a test failed, you made a mistake).
- **Saying no to a client or stakeholder:** they want something you can't give (more features, a faster date, a lower price).
- **Facing someone's anger:** a client or boss is already upset and you're in front of them.

Under pressure, many people **hide the news** in a long explanation. The listener gets more and more nervous, and when the bad news finally arrives, they're angry *and* confused. The better order is:

1. **Headline:** say the news in the first sentence. "I'll be straight with you: we're going to miss Friday."
2. **Ownership:** if it's your fault (or partly), say so quickly. "That's on me. I should've flagged it sooner." Don't over-apologise. One clear sentence is stronger than five "sorry"s.
3. **Short reason:** one or two sentences. Not an excuse. "The payment provider changed their system, and it broke our checkout."
4. **Options:** give two or three. People calm down when they have choices. "We can launch Friday without Apple Pay, or launch the full thing next Wednesday."
5. **Next step:** a clear action. "I'll send you both plans by two o'clock."

**When the other person is angry, don't argue with the feeling.** Name it, accept it, then move to action: "I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair. Let's figure out what we can do." Then **stop talking** and let them respond. Silence after an empathy sentence is powerful.

**Saying no: use "I can't… but I can…".** Never leave a "no" on its own. "I'm not able to add the reports by Friday, but I can get you a simple export by Friday and the full reports in two weeks."

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I'll be straight with you. | you're about to give bad news directly | "I'll be straight with you. The test didn't go well." |
| I wanted you to hear it from me first. | you tell your boss before they hear it elsewhere | "I wanted you to hear it from me first: the launch is slipping a week." |
| That's on me. | you take responsibility | "That's on me. I didn't check the final file." |
| I should've flagged it sooner. | you admit you waited too long | "I knew there was a risk. I should've flagged it sooner." |
| Here's what happened, in short. | you give a short reason, not a long excuse | "Here's what happened, in short: the API changed and broke our checkout." |
| I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair. | the other person is upset | "I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair. This isn't what we promised." |
| I get it. It's not what you wanted to hear. | you accept their reaction | "I get it. It's not what you wanted to hear, two days before the launch." |
| Here's what we can do. | you move from problem to options | "Here's what we can do. Option one…" |
| I'm not able to…, but I can… | you say no with an alternative | "I'm not able to change the whole layout, but I can fix the top section by Monday." |
| What I can commit to is… | you make a realistic promise | "What I can commit to is a working version by the twelfth." |
| Which matters more to you: …or…? | you let them choose the trade-off | "Which matters more to you: the date, or having every feature?" |
| Let's figure out the best option together. | you want to turn a fight into teamwork | "Let's figure out the best option together. Can I walk you through what we have?" |
| I understand why you'd see it that way. | they've misunderstood or blame you unfairly | "I understand why you'd see it that way. Can I give you the full picture?" |
| I'd rather tell you now than surprise you later. | you explain why you're raising it early | "It might be nothing, but I'd rather tell you now than surprise you later." |

### Examples

**Example 1: Bad news to your boss**

> **You:** Hey Maria, do you have five minutes? I wanted you to hear it from me first. The usability test for the new checkout didn't go well.
> **Maria:** Oh no. How bad?
> **You:** I'll be straight with you: four out of six people couldn't find the promo code field. That's on me. I moved it to save space and didn't test that change early enough. Here's what we can do: we can move it back, which is about a day of work, or try a new position and test it again on Thursday. I'd go with moving it back for the launch and testing the new idea later.
> **Maria:** OK. Thanks for telling me straight away. Let's move it back.

**Example 2: Saying no to a client**

> **Client (Frank):** We need the reporting dashboard in this release. Our board meeting is in two weeks.
> **You:** I get it, the board meeting is important. I'm not able to get the full dashboard done in two weeks without breaking something else. But what I can commit to is a one-page summary with the three numbers your board cares about most. Then the full dashboard in the next release. Which matters more to you: having it all, or having it on time?
> **Frank:** On time. The board doesn't need everything.
> **You:** Great, then let's agree on those three numbers this week.

**Example 3: Facing an angry client**

> **Client (Linda):** This is the second time the app has gone down this month. We're paying a lot of money for this!
> **You:** I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair. Twice in a month isn't OK.
> **Linda:** No, it's not.
> **You:** Here's what happened, in short: a server update failed, and the backup didn't switch on. The team fixed it in forty minutes. What I can commit to is a written report by Friday with what changed, so it doesn't happen again. Would it help if our engineering lead joined our next call to walk you through it?
> **Linda:** Yes. I want to hear it from them.

### Common mistakes

❌ "So, first, the payment provider changed some things, and then our engineers were testing, and then on Tuesday… so we will maybe be a little bit late."
✅ "I'll be straight with you: we're gonna miss Friday. Here's what happened, in short…"
*Headline first. Hiding the bad news makes it worse.*

❌ "Sorry, sorry, I'm really so sorry, it's very bad, I'm sorry…"
✅ "That's on me. Here's what we can do."
*One clear sentence of ownership, then action. Too many apologies sound like panic.*

❌ "Calm down, it's not a big problem."
✅ "I can hear that you're frustrated, and that's fair."
*Never tell an angry person to calm down. Name the feeling and accept it.*

❌ "No, it's impossible."
✅ "I'm not able to do it by Friday, but I can do this part by Friday."
*Always pair a "no" with what you can do.*

❌ "It's not my fault, the engineers didn't…"
✅ "Here's what happened…" *(facts, no blame)*
*Blaming others in front of a client hurts the whole team, including you.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min): Voice mode with Claude

Tonight Claude plays two tough characters. You stay calm, clear and honest. Open voice mode and paste this prompt:

```text
I'm a B2 English speaker working in product design, and I'm practising difficult conversations at work. Please run three role-play rounds with me. Be realistic and tough: interrupt me sometimes, push back, show frustration, but don't be abusive. Stay in character until I say "pause".

Round 1 (20 minutes): You are Diane, my boss, Head of Product. I have to tell you that the redesign of our mobile app's home screen will be two weeks late, because the engineers found that the new layout is too slow on older phones. You are under pressure from the CEO and you're not happy. Ask tough questions like "Why am I only hearing this now?" and "What exactly have you been doing?"

Round 2 (20 minutes): You are Mr. Carter, a client from a large retail company. You want us to add a loyalty-points feature to your app before your holiday sale in three weeks. It's not possible in three weeks without cutting testing. Push me hard to say yes. Mention how much money you're paying. If I give good options, slowly become more reasonable.

Round 3 (15 minutes): You are Sophie, a stakeholder from marketing. You are angry because a design change I made reduced newsletter sign-ups by 20 percent, and you found out from a report, not from me. Start the conversation angry.

After each round, step out of character and give me feedback in this order: (1) Did I give the headline first? (2) Did I take ownership without over-apologising? (3) Did I offer options and a next step? (4) Did I stay calm, or did I start to sound defensive? (5) Correct my 3 most important language mistakes and give me 2 more natural phrases I could have used. Then start the next round.
```

**What to focus on:**

- **Round 1:** headline in your first or second sentence. Use "I wanted you to hear it from me first" or "I'll be straight with you". When Diane asks "Why am I only hearing this now?", use "That's on me. I should've flagged it sooner." then move to options.
- **Round 2:** never say a flat "no". Use "I'm not able to…, but I can…" and "Which matters more to you…?" at least once each.
- **Round 3:** start with empathy and *stop talking* for a moment. Don't explain until Sophie has had her say. Then own the communication mistake, even if you think the design change was right. You can defend the design later (use your [Day 3](day-3-defending-an-idea.md) steps).
- **All rounds:** keep your voice low and slow, like tonight's shadowing.

**Last 15 minutes:** do your weakest round again. Say "Let's redo Round [number], same character, but even tougher." Record it if you can.

Write down every correction Claude gave you. You'll log them in the review.
