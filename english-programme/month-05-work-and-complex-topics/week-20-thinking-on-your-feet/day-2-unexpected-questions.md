## Before you start

- Yesterday you handled hard questions *about your talk*. Tonight the questions come from nowhere: in a meeting, a 1:1, an interview, a coffee chat. You haven't prepared, and the topic may not be yours.
- Reread [Week 11 Day 3: Talking when you're unsure](../../month-03-spontaneity/week-11-never-stopping/day-3-talking-when-youre-unsure.md). Tonight builds on "I'm not sure, but I think…" and turns it into a structured way to think out loud at work.
- **Book or plan live conversation #1** for tonight (40 minutes). Best options: a coffee chat or 1:1 with a colleague, a language exchange partner, or an online tutor. Tell them in advance: "Can you ask me some random, unexpected questions? I'm practising thinking on my feet." If you can't find anyone, use the voice-mode backup in Step 3 of the drill.

## Learning objectives

- React naturally to a question you didn't expect, instead of freezing or saying "umm".
- **Think out loud** in a structured way so the listener can follow you while you decide.
- **Narrow** a big or vague question before you answer it.
- Give a **provisional answer** ("my gut says…") and say what would change your mind.
- Start talking within 2 seconds, every time.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Focus: openers that buy time without sounding stuck.** When interviewees get a surprise question, good ones *show* the surprise for a second ("Ooh", "Huh", "Wow, OK") and then give a thinking frame ("Let me think about that for a sec"). The trick is the **melody**: the surprise word goes up and is stretched ("Oooh"), then the frame comes out flat and quick. It sounds relaxed, like the question is interesting, not scary.

| # | Sentence | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Ooh, that's a TOUGH one. | "OOOH, that's a TUFF one" (pitch up on "ooh") |
| 2 | Huh. Nobody's ever ASKED me that. | "HUH. nobody's ever ASKED me that" |
| 3 | Wow, OK. Let me think about that for a SEC. | "wow-o-KAY. lemme think about that fer a SEC" |
| 4 | That's a big QUESTION. | "that's a BIG QUES-chun" |
| 5 | Hmm, off the TOP of my head… | "hmmm, off the TOP of my head" |
| 6 | I've never really thought about it, but my GUT says no. | "I've never REALLY thought about it, but my GUT says NO" |
| 7 | Do you mean for US, or for the users? | "d'you mean fer US, or fer the USers?" |
| 8 | OK, I'm gonna think out loud for a second. | "o-kay, I'm gonna think out LOUD fer a second" |
| 9 | That's a good one. I'd probably say… DESIGN. | "that's a GOOD one. I'd PROB'ly say… de-ZINE" |
| 10 | Let me come at it from a different ANGLE. | "lemme COME at it from a DIFF'rent ANG-gul" |

**Clip to find:** "rapid-fire questions" segments, where guests get quick, surprising questions. Search YouTube for "rapid fire questions interview", "73 questions style interview", "lightning round podcast" or "this or that questions founder". Speak along with the guest's answers for 3–4 minutes, then pause-and-repeat the 3 openers you liked most. Method: [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Unexpected questions

### Explanation

An unexpected question is scary for one reason: your brain thinks it must produce the **perfect, finished answer** immediately. Native speakers don't do that. They do four things instead:

1. **React (1 second).** Show you heard it and it's interesting: "Ooh, good one." This is honest and it buys a second.
2. **Narrow it (optional).** Big or vague questions ("What do you think about AI?") are hard because they're too wide. Make them smaller: "Do you mean for our team, or in general?" Now you're answering a question you *can* answer.
3. **Think out loud with a frame.** Say what you're doing: "Let me think out loud for a second." Then give your thinking in steps: "On the one hand… but then again… so I guess…". The listener follows you, and your silence becomes speech.
4. **Land on a provisional answer.** "So my gut says yes, but I'd want to look at the numbers first." A provisional answer is a real answer. It's just honest about how sure you are.

At work, there are three kinds of unexpected question you'll meet often:

- **The "big picture" question from a senior person:** "If we had half the budget, what would you cut?" They want to see how you think, not a perfect answer.
- **The question outside your area:** "What do you think about the new pricing?" You can still give a user or design view.
- **The personal question in an interview or coffee chat:** "What's something you've changed your mind about recently?"

Notice the difference from Week 11: there you learned to keep going when unsure. Tonight you learn to make your thinking **organised and visible**, which sounds smart instead of lost.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Ooh, good one. / Ooh, that's a tough one. | you need a second and want to sound relaxed | "Ooh, that's a tough one. Give me a sec." |
| Huh, I've never really thought about that. | the question is new to you | "Huh, I've never really thought about that, but I think…" |
| Do you mean… or…? | the question is too wide | "Do you mean what I'd cut from the product, or from the team's work?" |
| Are you asking about… specifically? | you want to check the focus | "Are you asking about the mobile app specifically?" |
| Let me think out loud for a second. | you want your thinking to be visible | "Let me think out loud for a second. The obvious answer is…" |
| Off the top of my head… | you're giving a quick, unresearched answer | "Off the top of my head, I'd say the settings page." |
| My first instinct is… / My gut says… | you have an early opinion | "My gut says we should wait, but I could be wrong." |
| The obvious answer would be…, but actually… | you want to sound thoughtful | "The obvious answer would be the homepage, but actually I'd cut the reports." |
| If I had to pick one… | you need to choose | "If I had to pick one, it'd be search." |
| I'd want to check…, but my guess is… | you need data but still give an answer | "I'd want to check the data, but my guess is most people never use it." |
| What would change my mind is… | you want to show you're open | "What would change my mind is if the support tickets went up." |
| Can I come back to you on that? I want to give you a proper answer. | it really needs more thought | "Can I come back to you on that tomorrow? I want to give you a proper answer." |

### Examples

**Example 1: The senior person's big-picture question (in a planning meeting)**

> **Helen (VP of product):** Quick one for you. If we had half the design budget next year, what would you cut?
> **You:** Ooh, that's a tough one. Do you mean projects, or people's time?
> **Helen:** Projects.
> **You:** OK, let me think out loud for a second. The obvious answer would be the brand refresh, because it's the most expensive. But actually, I'd keep that and cut the admin redesign. Only about two hundred people use the admin tool, and they've learned to live with it. So if I had to pick one, it'd be the admin redesign. What would change my mind is if the support team told me it's causing a lot of tickets.
> **Helen:** Interesting. I wouldn't have guessed that.

**Example 2: Outside your area (coffee with an engineer)**

> **Raj:** What do you think about moving everything to the new database?
> **You:** Huh, that's not really my world, so take this with a grain of salt. From the design side, my first instinct is: as long as the app doesn't get slower, users won't care. Are you asking because it might affect loading times?
> **Raj:** Yeah, kind of.
> **You:** Then I'd want to check with you guys how much slower, but my guess is anything under a second is fine.

**Example 3: The personal interview question**

> **Interviewer:** What's something you've changed your mind about in the last year?
> **You:** Huh, I like that question. Off the top of my head… dark mode! I used to think it was a waste of time. Then we did research with nurses who use our app on night shifts, and they told us the bright screen was actually painful. So now I think it's an accessibility thing, not just a style thing.

### Common mistakes

❌ *(Three seconds of silence, then)* "Umm… I don't know… umm…"
✅ "Ooh, good one. Let me think out loud for a second…"
*Fill the first second with a reaction, not silence.*

❌ Answering a huge question with a huge, messy answer.
✅ "Do you mean for our team, or in general?" — then a focused answer.
*Narrow it first. Smaller questions are easier and the answer is better.*

❌ "Maybe yes, maybe no, it depends on many things."
✅ "My gut says yes, but I'd want to check the numbers."
*"It depends" without a direction isn't an answer. Give your lean, then the condition (see [Week 6 Day 5](../../month-02-speed/week-06-opinions/day-5-hedging-and-it-depends.md)).*

❌ "From the top of my head…"
✅ "Off the top of my head…"
*Fixed phrase: off the top of my head.*

❌ "I need to think. I will answer later." *(for a simple question)*
✅ "If I had to pick one right now, it'd be search."
*Save "Can I come back to you?" for questions that really need research. For opinion questions, give a quick answer.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

### Step 1: Rapid-fire surprise round (30 min)

This is your warm-up for tonight's live conversation.

1. **Round A (15 min).** Go through the 30 unexpected questions in [Exercise 2.4](exercises.md#exercise-24-30-unexpected-questions-drill-material). Read each one out loud, then answer it **within 2 seconds**, for 20–40 seconds. Use a different opener each time. Record the whole round without stopping.
2. **Round B: 3/2/1 (12 min).** Use the [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md) on question 7 from the list ("If you could change one thing about how your company works, what would it be?"). 3 minutes, 2 minutes, 1 minute. Think out loud in the 3-minute version; in the 1-minute version, give only the provisional answer and one reason.
3. **Quick listen (3 min).** Listen to any 5 answers from Round A. Did you start within 2 seconds? Mark yes/no for each.

### Step 2: Live conversation #1 (40 min)

**Your mission:** get the other person to ask you at least **five questions you didn't expect**, and answer each one with the react → narrow → think out loud → provisional answer pattern.

How to set it up: tell your partner, "I'm practising answering questions I'm not ready for. Can you throw me some random ones during our chat, about work, life, anything?" In a work coffee chat, you can start it yourself: "Ask me anything. What's something you've always wanted to know about the design team?"

Things to try:

1. Use "Ooh, good one" or "Huh, I've never really thought about that" at least twice.
2. Narrow at least one question with "Do you mean… or…?"
3. Say "Let me think out loud for a second" once and actually do it.
4. Ask *them* one surprise question back, from [Exercise 2.4](exercises.md#exercise-24-30-unexpected-questions-drill-material), and listen to how a native or fluent speaker opens their answer.
5. Record the conversation if your partner agrees.

More tips on finding partners and handling nerves: [Live conversations](../../toolkit/live-conversations.md).

**Backup plan: voice mode with Claude.** If your live conversation falls through, open voice mode and paste this:

```text
Let's have a relaxed 40-minute chat in English, like two colleagues having coffee. During the chat, every two or three minutes, throw me a question I won't expect: about work, design, my life, a strange "what would you do" question, or a big-picture question a senior manager might ask. Don't warn me before. After each of my answers, react naturally and keep the conversation going. Every 10 minutes, stop for 30 seconds and tell me: did I start my answers fast enough, did I narrow big questions, and give me one more natural opener I could have used. I'm a B2 speaker working in product design and I'm practising thinking on my feet.
```
