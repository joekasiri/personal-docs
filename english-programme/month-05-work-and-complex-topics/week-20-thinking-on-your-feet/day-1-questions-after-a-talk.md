## Before you start

- Reread [Week 16 Day 4: Answering questions in public](../../month-04-real-life-pressure/week-16-speaking-in-front-of-people/day-4-answering-questions-in-public.md). That lesson gave you the basics: repeat or rephrase the question, take a second, answer briefly, check the person is happy. Tonight assumes you can already do that. We go into the **hard** questions.
- Choose the work project you'll use all week (see the [week overview](README.md)). Write three bullet points about it on a card: what the problem was, what you made, what happened. Nothing more.
- Have your recording app ready and a timer.

## Learning objectives

- Recognise the six hard question types that come after a talk, a demo or a design review.
- Use a ready-made **first move** for each type, so you can start talking within two seconds.
- Answer with the **headline first**, then one reason, then stop.
- Say "I don't know" in a way that sounds professional, not weak.
- Stay friendly with a hostile question, and park an off-topic one without being rude.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**This week's focus: how interviewees open answers.** Listen to anyone good at interviews and you'll notice that they almost never start with the answer itself. They start with a short **opener** ("Yeah, so…", "Great question…", "Honestly…"), then a tiny pause, then the answer. The opener is said fast and a bit lower, and the pause after it is short (half a second). That pause is where their brain catches up. Tonight, copy the **opener + pause** pattern. Notice that the opener is not stressed; the stress lands on the first important word of the real answer.

Say each sentence 3 times with the rhythm marked. The "/" is the short pause.

| # | Sentence | How it sounds |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Yeah, so / the SHORT answer is no. | "yeah-so / the SHORT answer's NO" |
| 2 | Good question. / We actually TESTED that. | "good-question / we AK-chully TESTed that" |
| 3 | So there are two parts to that. / Let me start with the FIRST one. | "so there're TWO parts ta that / lemme start with the FIRST one" |
| 4 | Honestly? / I don't KNOW yet. | "HON-estly? / I dunno YET" |
| 5 | That's fair. / And it's something we WORRIED about too. | "that's-fair / 'n it's something we WORRied about too" |
| 6 | I'm gonna be honest, / that one SURPRISED us. | "I'm-gonna-be-honest / that one sur-PRISED us" |
| 7 | OK, so / quick CONTEXT first. | "o-kay-so / quick CON-text first" |
| 8 | Right, / so the reason we did it is SPEED. | "right / so the REAson we did it is SPEED" |
| 9 | I'd push back on that a LITTLE. | "I'd PUSH back on that a LIL" |
| 10 | Great point, / and I'd LOVE to come back to it after. | "great point / 'n I'd LOVE ta come back to it after" |

**Clip to find:** a conference Q&A or a podcast interview where a guest answers questions from the host or the audience. Search YouTube for "design conference Q&A", "product talk audience questions", "founder interview podcast" or "tech talk questions from the audience". Choose a speaker who sounds relaxed, not one reading from notes.

**How:** speak along with 3–4 minutes of the answers, without pausing the clip. Then pick the 3 hardest openers you heard, use pause-and-repeat on just those, and copy the exact pitch. Full method: [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Questions after a talk

### Explanation

In Week 16 you learned to answer friendly questions in front of people. In real work, the questions after a talk, a demo or a design review are often **not** friendly or simple. There are six types that cause most of the freezing:

1. **The multi-part question.** Someone asks three things at once. You panic, forget the second part, and answer only the last one.
2. **The challenge (hostile) question.** "Isn't this just a copy of what our competitor did?" There's an attack inside it. Your body reacts before your English does.
3. **The off-topic question.** A good question, but about something else, and answering it would eat the whole meeting.
4. **The question you can't answer.** You don't have the data, or it's not your area.
5. **The comment that isn't a question.** Someone talks for a minute, gives their opinion, and then stops. Everyone looks at you.
6. **The loaded question.** The question contains a wrong idea: "Why did you decide to ignore accessibility?" (You didn't ignore it.)

The good news: every type has a **first move**, a sentence you can say without thinking, that buys you two seconds and shows you're in control. After the first move, the rule is always the same: **headline first, one reason, stop.** Non-native speakers often do the opposite: they give the background first and arrive at the answer after a minute. Under pressure, that's when you lose your train of thought. Say the answer in the first sentence, then explain.

**"I don't know" is allowed.** It sounds weak only when it's the *whole* answer. It sounds professional when you add (a) what you *do* know, and (b) when you'll find out: "I don't have that number with me, but I'll send it to you by Friday."

**With a hostile question, agree with the part that's true.** You don't need to win. Acknowledge the concern, then give your answer calmly. A calm answer to a hostile question makes *you* look good, whatever you actually say.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So there are a couple of things in there. | it's a multi-part question | "So there are a couple of things in there. Let me take them one at a time." |
| Let me start with… and then come back to… | you want to order the parts | "Let me start with the timeline, and then come back to the budget question." |
| Remind me, what was the second part? | you've genuinely forgotten part of it | "OK, that's the first part. Sorry, remind me, what was the second part?" |
| That's a fair challenge. | the question has an attack in it | "That's a fair challenge. We did look at what they did, and here's where we're different." |
| I can see why it looks that way. | the question is based on a wrong impression | "I can see why it looks that way from the outside. What actually happened was…" |
| I'd push back on that a little. | you need to correct the idea inside a loaded question | "I'd push back on that a little. We didn't skip accessibility. We tested it in week two." |
| That's a great question, and it's a bit outside what I covered today. | it's off-topic | "That's a great question, and it's a bit outside what I covered today. Can we grab five minutes after?" |
| Can we take that offline? | you want to discuss it later, privately | "That's a big one. Can we take that offline? I don't want to rush it." |
| I don't have that in front of me, but… | you're missing data | "I don't have the exact number in front of me, but it was somewhere around twenty percent." |
| I'll find out and get back to you by… | you promise a real follow-up | "I'll check with the data team and get back to you by Thursday." |
| That's not really my area, but [name] would know. | someone else is the expert | "That's not really my area, but Priya on the platform team would know." |
| Thanks for that. If I understand your point… | someone made a comment, not a question | "Thanks for that. If I understand your point, you're saying it's too early to scale?" |
| The short answer is… The longer answer is… | you want headline first | "The short answer is yes. The longer answer is yes, but only on mobile for now." |
| Does that answer your question? | you've finished and want to close the loop | "…so that's why we waited. Does that answer your question?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: The multi-part question (after a design review of a new checkout flow)**

> **Marco (engineering lead):** So, a few things. How long will this take to build, did you test it with older users, and what happens to the saved-cards feature?
> **You:** OK, so there are three things in there. Let me take them one at a time. Build time: the short answer is about three sprints. Older users: yes, we had four people over sixty in the last round, and they did fine. And saved cards… that one stays exactly the same, we didn't touch it.
> **Marco:** OK, great.
> **You:** Does that cover everything?

**Example 2: The challenge question**

> **Dana (sales director):** Honestly, isn't this just the same dashboard our competitor launched last year?
> **You:** That's a fair challenge. We did look at theirs, and I can see why it looks similar at first glance. The big difference is the alerts. Theirs shows you the data; ours tells you when something's wrong, so you don't have to check. In testing, that was the thing people liked most.
> **Dana:** Hmm, OK. I'd want to see that in the demo.
> **You:** Totally. I'll make sure it's front and center.

**Example 3: The question you can't answer + the off-topic question**

> **Leo (product manager):** What's the conversion rate on the new onboarding so far?
> **You:** Good question. I don't have the exact number in front of me, but from what I saw on Monday it was up, around fifteen percent. I'll check with the data team and send you the real number by tomorrow.
> **Sam (marketing):** And while we're here, are we changing the brand colours next year?
> **You:** Ooh, that's a great question, and it's a bit outside what I covered today. Can we take that offline? I'll grab you after this.

**Example 4: The comment that isn't a question**

> **Ruth (head of support):** I just think we're moving too fast. Every time we change the layout, our support tickets go up for a month, and nobody ever plans for that. So, yeah.
> **You:** Thanks for that, Ruth, that's really useful. If I understand your point, you're saying we need a plan for support *before* we launch, not after?
> **Ruth:** Exactly.
> **You:** Totally agree. Let's add it to the launch checklist. Could you and I spend twenty minutes on it this week?

### Common mistakes

❌ "Umm… so, first, the project started in March, and the team was…" (answer to "Did it work?")
✅ "Yes, mostly. The sign-ups went up, but retention didn't move. Here's why…"
*Headline first. Background only if they need it.*

❌ "I don't know." *(and then silence)*
✅ "I don't know yet. We're measuring it now, and I'll have numbers by the end of the month."
*Always add what you know or when you'll know.*

❌ "No, that's wrong. We didn't copy anyone."
✅ "I can see why it looks that way. The difference is…"
*Defending hard makes a hostile question stronger. Calm acknowledgement takes the heat out.*

❌ "Sorry, can you repeat all the question?"
✅ "OK, I got the first two parts. Remind me, what was the last one?"
*Ask only for the part you missed. It shows you were listening.*

❌ "That is not the topic of my presentation."
✅ "That's a great question, and it's a bit outside what I covered today. Can we grab five minutes after?"
*Same message, but friendly. Never make someone feel stupid for asking.*

❌ "I will revert to you." / "I will come back to you with the answer."
✅ "I'll get back to you on that by Thursday."
*"Get back to you" is the natural phrase, and adding a day makes it a real promise.*

### Practise

Go to [Day 1 exercises](exercises.md#day-1-exercises) and do them in this order:

1. **Exercise 1.1: Name it, then answer it** (7 min): identify the question type and say the first move.
2. **Exercise 1.2: Headline first** (6 min): turn background-first answers into headline-first answers, out loud.
3. **Exercise 1.3: Fix the reply** (7 min): correct weak or rude answers and say the better version 3 times.

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

Tonight you give a short talk about your project, then face a room full of difficult people (played by your own question list).

**Step 1: Warm-up random topic (10 min).** Use the [random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md). Pick 4 topics from the "hard" group of the [topic cards](../../toolkit/random-topics.md), or use these: *a meeting that went badly · the best presentation you've ever seen · a question that surprised you · a tool you can't work without*. Speak 2 minutes on each, start immediately, record.

**Step 2: 3/2/1 mini talk (20 min).** Use the [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md). Prompt: **"Present your project to a group of people from other teams: the problem, what you designed, and the result."** Imagine a meeting room with eight people from sales, engineering and support. Give it in 3 minutes, then 2, then 1. Record all three. In the 1-minute version, your first sentence must be the headline ("We redesigned X, and it did Y").

**Step 3: Q&A role-play (30 min).** Straight after the 1-minute version, start the Q&A. Go through the **12 tough questions** in [Exercise 1.4 in the Day 1 exercises](exercises.md#exercise-14-the-12-tough-questions-drill-material). For each one:

1. Read it out loud, as if a colleague is asking it (use a slightly annoyed voice for the hostile ones).
2. Start your answer **within 2 seconds** with a first move from tonight's table.
3. Answer in 30–60 seconds: headline, one reason, stop.
4. End with a check: "Does that answer your question?" or "Does that make sense?" (from [Week 7 Day 5](../../month-02-speed/week-07-explaining/day-5-checking-understanding.md)).

Record all 12 answers in one file. Don't stop the recording between questions. If you freeze, use a time-buyer from [Week 3 Day 4](../../month-01-foundations/week-03-small-talk/day-4-time-buyers.md) and keep going. Then do questions 3, 6 and 9 a second time, faster.

**Step 4: Listen back (10 min).** Listen to the Q&A recording. Count your [3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md) for the first 2 minutes of it. Then check: in how many of the 12 answers was the headline in the first sentence? Write that number in your notes. Aim for 9 out of 12.

## 4. Review (15 min)

Spaced review, out loud. Follow the schedule in [Phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md).

1. **Log 5 new phrases from tonight** (3 min). Suggested: "So there are a couple of things in there", "I'd push back on that a little", "Can we take that offline?", "I'll get back to you on that by…", "The short answer is…".
2. **Yesterday's phrases** (4 min): your phrases from [Week 19 Day 6](../week-19-complex-topics/day-6-weekly-check.md). Say each one in a new sentence about tonight's project.
3. **Last week's phrases** (4 min): [Week 19 Day 1: News and current events](../week-19-complex-topics/day-1-news-and-current-events.md). Say each one in a sentence.
4. **Last month's phrases** (4 min): [Week 16 Day 4: Answering questions in public](../../month-04-real-life-pressure/week-16-speaking-in-front-of-people/day-4-answering-questions-in-public.md). Notice how tonight's phrases build on those.

Write your numbers and the headline-first score in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).
