## Before you start

- You know the [12 storytelling connectors](day-1-storytelling-connectors.md) and the [reaction phrases](day-2-reaction-phrases.md). Tonight they slot into a structure.
- Have ready: phone for recording, timer, a piece of paper folded into 4 boxes (you'll use it in the exercises).
- Think of a funny or embarrassing thing that happened to you (for the drill). Everyone has one.

## Learning objectives

- Link a final consonant to the next vowel, so "pick it up" sounds like "pi-ki-dup".
- Tell any story in 4 parts: **setting → problem → turning point → ending**.
- Keep each part short — 1 to 3 sentences — so the story doesn't drag.
- Know which connectors fit naturally in each part.

## 1. Shadowing (20 min)

### Tonight's sound: linking consonant → vowel

When a word **ends in a consonant** and the next word **starts with a vowel sound**, Americans move the consonant across to the next word. There's no gap. "Pick it up" isn't three separate words — it's one smooth chunk: **"pi-ki-dup"**. (The T in "it up" becomes a flap D, like on [Day 1](day-1-storytelling-connectors.md#1-shadowing-20-min).)

If you say every word separately — "pick. it. up." — you sound like a robot, and you also speak more slowly. Linking makes you faster without any extra effort.

| Written | Linked | |
|---|---|---|
| pick it up | pi-ki-DUP | "pickidup" |
| check it out | che-ki-DOUT | "checkidout" |
| turn it off | tur-ni-DOFF | "turnidoff" |
| come on in | co-mo-NIN | "comonin" |
| not at all | na-da-DALL | "nadadall" |
| an apple | a-NAP-ple | "anapple" |

### Practice sentences

Say each 3 times. CAPITALS = stressed. Hyphens show where the sounds join.

1. Can you pick it UP? → "Can you pi-ki-DUP?"
2. You gotta check it OUT. → "You gotta che-ki-DOUT."
3. Turn it OFF for a sec. → "Tur-ni-DOFF for a sec."
4. Come on IN. → "Co-mo-NIN."
5. It's not a big DEAL. → "It's na-da big DEAL."
6. Hold on a SECond. → "Hol-do-na SECond."
7. Think about it. → "Thin-ka-bou-DIT."
8. I'm on it. → "I'-mo-NIT."
9. She looked at us. → "She look-ta-DUS."
10. Let's get out of HERE. → "Let's ge-dout-ta HERE."
11. I ended up in an Uber. → "I en-de-du-pi-na-NUber."
12. Give it a TRY. → "Gi-vi-da TRY."

### Find a clip

Pause-and-repeat ([Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md)).

- Search YouTube for **"funny storytime"**, **"embarrassing moments storytime"**, **"most awkward thing that happened to me"**.
- Pick 4–5 sentences that sound "fast" to you. Usually they're fast because of linking. Write one down, mark the links with hyphens, then repeat it 5 times.
- 8 minutes on the sentences above, 12 on the clip.

## 2. Main skill (40 min): Story structure

### Explanation

When you're nervous, two things often happen to your stories: they **start too slowly** (too much background), or they **never really end** ("…and yeah. So. That's it."). Both make you freeze, because you don't know where you are in the story.

The fix is a simple map. Almost every good casual story has **4 parts**:

| Part | What it does | How long |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Setting** | When, where, who. Just enough for the listener to picture it. | 1–2 sentences |
| **2. Problem** | What went wrong, or what was unusual. This is why the story is worth telling. | 1–3 sentences |
| **3. Turning point** | The moment something changed: a surprise, a decision, someone said something. | 1–3 sentences |
| **4. Ending** | What happened in the end, plus a short comment: how you felt, or what you learned. | 1–2 sentences |

That's it. **About 6–10 sentences.** If you know the four boxes, you always know what comes next, and your brain doesn't have to plan while you talk.

**Keep it short.** A casual story is not a report. You don't need the date, the full names, or what you had for breakfast. Ask yourself: *does the listener need this to understand the problem?* If not, cut it.

**Connectors fit the boxes:**

- Setting: *So basically… · So this one time… · This was back when…*
- Problem: *The thing is… · And then out of nowhere…*
- Turning point: *At that point… · Turns out… · I was like… · And she goes…*
- Ending: *Long story short… · In the end… · The funny part is… · So yeah, lesson learned.*

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So this one time… | you start a story about one event | "So this one time, I fell asleep on the train and ended up in a different city." |
| This was back when… | you set the time in the past | "This was back when I was still in college." |
| So I was at… | you set the place quickly | "So I was at my friend's wedding, right?" |
| Everything was fine until… | you move from setting to problem | "Everything was fine until the DJ played my name on the mic." |
| That's when… | you mark the turning point | "That's when I realized I had the wrong address." |
| So I decided to… | you made a choice at the turning point | "So I decided to just walk in and pretend it was normal." |
| Luckily… / Unfortunately… | the turning point goes well or badly | "Luckily, a nice old man helped me." |
| So yeah, lesson learned. | you finish with a short moral | "So yeah, lesson learned: always check the address." |
| Anyway, that's the story of… | you close the story clearly | "Anyway, that's the story of how I met my landlord." |
| I still think about it. / I'll never forget it. | you finish with a feeling | "Honestly, I'll never forget his face." |

### Examples

**Model story: embarrassing (about 45 seconds)**

> **Setting:** So this one time, I was on a video call with a new client — like, a really important one.
> **Problem:** Everything was fine until I thought I'd turned my camera off and started eating a huge sandwich. The thing is, my camera was very much on.
> **Turning point:** At that point the client goes, "Looks good! What's in it?" And I was like… frozen. Mouth full.
> **Ending:** Long story short, we ended up talking about sandwiches for five minutes, and they signed the contract. So yeah, lesson learned: check your camera.

**Model story: travel (about 40 seconds)**

> **Setting:** This was back when I was traveling in Portugal with two friends.
> **Problem:** We missed the last bus out of this tiny village, and there were no taxis. Like, none.
> **Turning point:** That's when this old guy at the café goes, "My son drives to the city at nine. He can take you."
> **Ending:** In the end, we rode in the back of a fish truck for an hour. The funny part is, it's my favorite memory of the whole trip.

**Too long vs short — same story:**

> ❌ *"So, OK, it was a Tuesday, no, Wednesday, in March, or April, and I had woken up early because my alarm, which is on my old phone, the one with the broken screen…"*
> ✅ *"So this one time, my alarm didn't go off on the morning of a big presentation."*

### Common mistakes

| | |
|---|---|
| ❌ "The story is about when I lost my passport." | ✅ "**So this one time**, I lost my passport." — *"The story is about…" sounds like a school essay.* |
| ❌ 1 minute of background before anything happens | ✅ 1–2 sentences of setting, then **straight to the problem** — *the listener waits for the problem.* |
| ❌ "…and then I went home. And yeah. That's it. So…" | ✅ "**In the end**, I got home at 3 a.m. **So yeah, lesson learned.**" — *give a clear ending so the listener knows to react.* |
| ❌ Telling the ending first: "So I got fired, but first…" | ✅ Keep the result for the end — *the turning point needs a little suspense.* |
| ❌ Every detail: names, dates, times, prices | ✅ Only details that help the listener **picture** it or **feel** it. |

## 3. Speaking drill (45 min)

### Part A: 4/3/2 with a funny or embarrassing story (20 min)

Follow [The 4/3/2 drill](../../toolkit/drill-4-3-2.md).

**Prompt:**
> **"Tell me about a time you embarrassed yourself."**
> Ideas if you're stuck: you waved at someone who wasn't waving at you; you called someone by the wrong name; you said something on a call when you thought you were on mute; you walked confidently into the wrong room; you laughed at the wrong moment.

1. 30 seconds: draw 4 boxes on paper. Write **one word** in each box (for example: *call · sandwich · "what's in it?" · signed*).
2. Round 1: 4 min. You have time — add description and feelings inside each box.
3. Round 2: 3 min.
4. Round 3: 2 min. Every box still has to be there. Cut details, not boxes.
5. Count your 3 numbers on round 3 and write them in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).

### Part B: Check the structure (10 min)

Listen to round 3. On paper, write the time when each box starts (for example: setting 0:00, problem 0:15, turning point 0:50, ending 1:40). Ask yourself:

- Did the setting take more than 20 seconds? → Cut it next time.
- Was there a clear turning point? → If not, decide what it is.
- Did you finish with a clear ending phrase? → If not, choose one now.

### Part C: The same story for a different listener (15 min)

Do a quick 3/2 (3 minutes, then 2 minutes) of the **same story**, but imagine you're telling it to a **new colleague at lunch** instead of a close friend. Keep the four boxes. Use at least **one connector in every box**.

## 4. Review (15 min)

1. **Reread yesterday's 5 phrases** (Day 2 reactions). Say each one in a new mini-situation, with big tone.
2. **Log 5 new phrases** in your [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md): pick from tonight's key phrases (good choices: *So this one time…*, *That's when…*, *So yeah, lesson learned.*). Write your own sentence for each.
3. Fill in tonight's row in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).

## Expected outcome

By the end of tonight you can…

- [ ] link words like "pick it up" and "check it out" smoothly
- [ ] name the 4 boxes of a story and what goes in each
- [ ] start a story in one sentence with "So this one time…"
- [ ] finish a story clearly, so the listener knows when to react
