## Before you start

- In Month 2 you learned how to give your own opinion, agree, disagree and back it up. Don't relearn those — just reread the key phrases in [giving opinions](../../month-02-speed/week-06-opinions/day-1-giving-opinions.md), [disagreeing politely](../../month-02-speed/week-06-opinions/day-3-disagreeing-politely.md) and [giving reasons and examples](../../month-02-speed/week-06-opinions/day-4-giving-reasons-and-examples.md) for 3 minutes.
- Tonight's new challenge: argue for an opinion you **don't** hold. It's the ultimate "no preparation" role-play: you have no ready-made thoughts, so you have to build them while you speak.
- Open [Day 5 exercises](exercises.md#day-5-exercises).

## Learning objectives

- Argue for a side you don't agree with, for 1–2 minutes, without stopping.
- Find the best reasons for the other side fast, using a simple 3-question trick.
- Use "devil's advocate" phrases to signal that you're playing a role.
- Sound confident and firm, even when you don't believe what you're saying.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's sound: confident and firm.** Unsure speakers end statements with a rising tone ↗, as if asking permission: "I think remote work is better?" Confident speakers use a **falling tone** ↘ at the end of each idea, a short pause after the key point, and strong stress on the important word. They don't rush.

Tips:
- Let your voice **drop** at the end of each sentence.
- **Pause** for half a second after your main point. Silence sounds confident; "umm" doesn't.
- Stress the word that carries your argument: "That's EXACTLY my point."

| # | Sentence | Stress and tone |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | OK, hear me out. | OK, HEAR me OUT. ↘ |
| 2 | Let me play devil's advocate for a sec. | lemme PLAY DEVil's ADvocate fer a SEC ↘ |
| 3 | Here's the thing. | HERE'S the THING. ↘ (pause) |
| 4 | That's exactly my point. | that's eg-ZACT-ly my POINT. ↘ |
| 5 | Think about it this way. | THINK about it THIS way. ↘ |
| 6 | Even if that's true, it doesn't change anything. | EVen if that's TRUE, it DOESn't CHANGE ANything. ↘ |
| 7 | What people forget is the cost. | what PEOple for-GET is the COST. ↘ |
| 8 | I'm not saying it's perfect. I'm saying it's better. | I'm NOT saying it's PERfect. I'm saying it's BETter. ↘ |
| 9 | At the end of the day, it's about time. | at the END of the DAY, it's about TIME. ↘ |
| 10 | And honestly? That matters more. | and HONestly? ↗ (pause) that MATTERS MORE. ↘ |

**Clip to find:** a friendly debate between two hosts, or a "hot takes" segment where people defend unpopular opinions. Search YouTube for **"unpopular opinions podcast"**, **"friendly debate two hosts"** or **"hot takes debate show"**.

**How:** speak along with the whole clip, no pausing, three times. Copy the falling tone and the pauses before big points. Method: [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (30 min): Arguing the other side

### Explanation

Why argue for something you don't believe? Three reasons:

1. **It's pure spontaneity.** Your own opinions are half-rehearsed in your head. The other side isn't. You have to think and speak at the same time — exactly the "talking without thinking" skill.
2. **It happens in real life.** At work you defend the team's decision even when you voted against it. With friends, someone says "OK, convince me", and you play along.
3. **It builds flexible vocabulary.** You'll need words and reasons you never use.

**The 3-question trick (takes 2 seconds in your head):** when you get a side you don't agree with, ask yourself:
- **Who benefits?** ("Who would love this?")
- **What's the hidden cost of the other side?** ("What's bad about *my* real opinion?")
- **What's one real example?** (a person, a story, a product)

Now you have three things to say. Start talking before you feel ready.

**Signal the role.** Use a phrase that tells people you're playing devil's advocate, so they don't think it's your real view: "OK, let me play devil's advocate…", "Just to argue the other side…". Then commit fully. Half-hearted arguing ("I guess, maybe, it could be…") sounds weak. Use the confident tone from shadowing.

**Handle the push-back.** If someone attacks your argument, don't drop it. Use: "Sure, but here's the thing…", "Even if that's true…", "I'm not saying… I'm saying…". These let you keep your position while you accept a small part of theirs.

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| OK, let me play devil's advocate. | Showing you're arguing the other side on purpose | "OK, let me play devil's advocate. What if meetings are actually good?" |
| Just to argue the other side… | Softer version of the same thing | "Just to argue the other side, cities are kinda great for kids." |
| Hear me out. | Before an idea people won't like | "Hear me out: pineapple on pizza is the best topping." |
| Think about it this way. | Giving a new angle | "Think about it this way: you'd save two hours a day." |
| Here's the thing. | Getting to your strongest point | "Here's the thing. Nobody reads the long version." |
| What people forget is… | Pointing out a hidden fact | "What people forget is how expensive that is." |
| Sure, but… / Fair, but… | Accepting a point and continuing | "Sure, but that's true for everything." |
| Even if that's true… | Their point doesn't change your conclusion | "Even if that's true, it's still worth it." |
| I'm not saying…, I'm saying… | Making your claim more precise | "I'm not saying it's perfect, I'm saying it's better than the alternative." |
| That's exactly my point. | Using their argument for your side | "It's hard? That's exactly my point. Hard things teach you." |
| At the end of the day… | Final summary point | "At the end of the day, people want it simple." |
| OK, I've gotta be honest, I don't actually believe that. | Dropping the role at the end | "OK, I've gotta be honest, I don't actually believe that. But it was fun." |

### Examples

**Model answer 1: "Working from home is worse than working in an office."** (Speaker actually loves remote work.)

> "OK, let me play devil's advocate here, because I *love* working from home. But hear me out. Think about it this way: when you're in the office, you learn stuff without trying. You overhear the engineers talking about a bug, you see how your manager handles a tough client. At home, you get none of that. And here's the thing, especially for junior people: they never get those little moments. What people forget is that a lot of good ideas come from random chats at the coffee machine, not from scheduled calls. Sure, you save time on the commute, but you lose so much more. At the end of the day, work is a team sport, and it's hard to be a team through a screen."

**Model answer 2: "Social media has made the world better."** (Speaker usually thinks it's bad.)

> "Just to argue the other side… I'm not saying social media is perfect. I'm saying it's better than nothing. Think about my grandma — she sees pictures of her grandkids every single day, and they live on different continents. Twenty years ago, she'd get a letter twice a year. And small businesses! A friend of mine sells ceramics, and like ninety percent of her customers find her on Instagram. Even if people waste time scrolling, that's on us, not the app. So yeah, at the end of the day, it connects people who would never be connected otherwise."

**Dialogue: holding your position under push-back**

> **Friend:** Come on, nobody really thinks cash is better than cards.
> **You:** OK, hear me out. With cash, you actually *feel* the money leaving. You spend less.
> **Friend:** But it's so inconvenient.
> **You:** Sure, but that's exactly my point. It's supposed to be a little annoying. That's what stops you buying stuff you don't need.
> **Friend:** What about safety? You can lose it.
> **You:** Even if that's true, you can lose a card too, and then someone empties your account. I'm not saying go full cash. I'm saying use it for the everyday stuff.
> **Friend:** Do you really believe that?
> **You:** Haha, honestly? Not at all. I haven't had cash in my wallet in like a year.

### Common mistakes

| ❌ | ✅ | Why |
|---|---|---|
| I don't agree with this, but maybe it's possibly a bit good? | OK, let me play devil's advocate. It's actually really good, and here's why. | Signal the role once, then commit. Half-hearted sounds weak. |
| Hear me. | Hear me out. | "Hear me out" is the fixed phrase ("listen until I finish"). |
| Even it's true… | Even if that's true… | You need "if". |
| I'm not say it's perfect. | I'm not saying it's perfect. | Present continuous: "I'm not saying…". |
| Yes, but no, because… | Sure, but here's the thing… | A smoother way to accept and push back. |
| (rising tone) It's better for the planet? ↗ | It's better for the planet. ↘ | Rising tone sounds like you're asking permission. |

## 3. Speaking drill (60 min)

Tonight: **random-topic drill, "wrong side" version**, plus one 3/2/1 round. Method: [Random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md).

**Step 1 – Warm-up (5 min).** Say all 12 key phrases once in a confident, falling tone. Then say "I'm not saying… I'm saying…" with three different endings.

**Step 2 – Random-topic rounds (35 min).** Shuffle these 14 statement cards. Pick one. In 2 seconds, decide which side you *don't* agree with — and argue **that** side for 1–2 minutes. Start with a devil's advocate phrase, use the 3-question trick, include one example and finish with "At the end of the day…". Record every round.

1. Everyone should learn to code.
2. Cats are better pets than dogs.
3. It's better to rent than to buy a home.
4. Kids shouldn't have smartphones until they're 16.
5. Open-plan offices are good for teams.
6. Breakfast is the most important meal of the day.
7. You should never lend money to friends.
8. AI tools make designers better at their job.
9. Living in a big city is better than living in a small town.
10. It's fine to cancel plans at the last minute.
11. Tourists should pay more than locals for museums.
12. Video calls are better than phone calls.
13. A four-day work week would be bad for most companies.
14. Learning a language with apps is a waste of time.

If you have time left, pick 3 cards again and argue your **real** opinion. Notice: does it feel easier now?

**Step 3 – One 3/2/1 round (15 min).** Pick the card from step 2 that was hardest. Argue the same "wrong" side in 3 minutes, then 2, then 1. In each version, include one push-back you imagine someone saying, and answer it ("Some people would say… Sure, but…"). Method: [3/2/1 drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md).

**Step 4 – Listen back and count (5 min).** Count your [3 numbers](../../toolkit/the-3-numbers.md) on the 1-minute version. Also check: how many sentences ended with a rising tone? Aim for zero.
