## Before you start

- Month 2 was all about **speed**: getting faster. Now that you're fast, tonight is about **control**: choosing when to go fast and when to slow down. Fast is not the same as fluent.
- Tonight pulls the whole week together: speed control only works with thought groups ([Day 2](day-2-thought-groups-and-pausing.md)), clear stress ([Day 1](day-1-stress-changes-meaning.md)) and clear sounds ([Day 4](day-4-tricky-american-sounds.md)).
- **Live conversation #2 is tonight.**
- You'll need a timer and your recording app.

## Learning objectives

- Measure your own speaking speed in **words per minute**.
- Slow down for important points and speed up on easy, familiar chunks.
- Notice when nerves make you rush, and use "brake phrases" to slow down.
- Tell the same content at three speeds and stay clear at all of them.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min): speed changes inside one clip

Good speakers don't have one speed. They **accelerate** through background details and familiar chunks ("so-I-was-like", "you-know-what-I-mean") and **brake** for the important part. Tonight, copy those gear changes.

Say each line with the speed shown. **[fast]** = squash it together, **[slow]** = stretch it, one word at a time.

1. **[fast]** "So-I-was-on-my-way-to-work-right," **[slow]** "and I SEE… my BOSS… on a SCOOTER."
2. **[fast]** "Anyway-long-story-short," **[slow]** "we GOT the contract."
3. **[fast]** "I-mean-I-don't-know-maybe-it's-just-me, but" **[slow]** "I think this is a really BIG deal."
4. **[slow]** "Here's the most IMPORTANT part." **[fast]** "Everything-else-is-kinda-whatever."
5. **[fast]** "It-was-like-nine-thirty-ten-o'clock," **[slow]** "and the lights went OUT."
6. **[slow]** "Do NOT… press… that button." **[fast]** "Seriously-it-deletes-everything."
7. **[fast]** "You-know-what-I-mean?" **[slow]** "It just… doesn't make SENSE."
8. **[slow]** "Three things." **[fast]** "Quick-ones-I-promise."

**Find a clip:** a stand-up comedian or an energetic storyteller, who changes speed all the time. Search for "stand up comedy storytelling", "fast talker storytime", "storytelling show live audience". Speak along, and notice: where does the speaker suddenly slow down? Usually just before or on the most important part. See [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Controlling your speed

### Explanation

**How fast is normal?** Relaxed American conversation is usually around **130–170 words per minute**. Faster when people are excited or telling a funny story, slower when explaining something important. A news presenter is often around 150. If you're much faster than 170, people may lose you, especially with an accent. If you're under 100, you might sound hesitant.

**How to measure yours:** record yourself talking for exactly 1 minute about something easy. Write down (or use a transcription app to get) every word you said and count them. That number is your words per minute (WPM). Do it again with something hard or stressful and compare.

**Why nervous speakers rush.** When you're nervous, you want the moment to be over, so you speed up, stop pausing, and your sounds get blurry. Ironically, that makes people ask "Sorry, what?", which makes you more nervous. The fix is **control**, not just "slow down":

1. **Accelerate on the easy parts:** familiar chunks, background details, fillers like "you know", "I mean". These are supposed to be fast and squashed.
2. **Brake on the important parts:** the key fact, the number, the name, the decision, the punchline. Slow down, stress clearly, maybe pause before it.
3. **Use brake phrases** when you feel yourself rushing. These phrases naturally slow you down and give you a breath: "So here's the thing…", "Let me put it this way…", "OK, so…", "The main point is…".
4. **Breathe at thought-group boundaries.** If you're out of breath, you're not pausing enough.

**Numbers and names always brake.** Whenever you say a number, a date, a price or a name, slow down a little. These are the things people mishear most (fifteen/fifty, you learned this on the phone in [Week 15](../../month-04-real-life-pressure/week-15-calls/README.md)).

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| So here's the thing… | braking before the key point | "So here's the thing… we don't have the budget." |
| Let me put it this way… | slowing down to rephrase clearly | "Let me put it this way: it's not ready." |
| The main point is… | slowing down for the summary | "The main point is, users can't find the button." |
| OK, so… | resetting your speed when you've been rushing | "OK, so… let me start again." |
| Long story short… | accelerating past details you don't need | "Long story short, we missed the flight." |
| Anyway… | speeding past a side story | "Anyway, that's not important." |
| Just to be clear… | slowing down for something important | "Just to be clear, the deadline is FRIday." |
| Sorry, I'm talking way too fast. | catching yourself rushing | "Sorry, I'm talking way too fast. I'm just excited." |
| Bear with me. | asking for patience while you explain slowly | "Bear with me, this part's a little complicated." |
| Two things, real quick. | promising a fast, short point | "Two things, real quick, then I'll let you go." |

### Examples

**Example 1: Gear changes in an update**

> "So, quick update. *(fast)* We tested the new checkout with five users last week, pretty standard. *(fast)* And here's the thing. *(brake)* Four out of five… couldn't find the… PAY button. *(slow, clear)* So, long story short, *(fast)* we're moving it to the top of the screen."

**Example 2: Catching yourself**

> A: So how was the trip?
> B: Oh-my-god-it-was-amazing-we-went-to-like-five-cities-and-the-food-was-insane-and-
> B: Sorry, I'm talking way too fast. OK, so… *(slower)* the best part was Kyoto. We stayed in this tiny old house… with a garden.

**Example 3: Numbers and names brake**

> "The meeting's at… four-FIFTEEN, in room… two-oh-SIX. And ask for… Mar-IS-sa. M-A-R-I-S-S-A."

### Common mistakes

❌ Speaking faster and faster when you're nervous.
✅ Using a brake phrase ("OK, so…") and taking a breath.
*Rushing makes you harder to understand, which makes you more nervous.*

❌ Saying important numbers as fast as everything else.
✅ Slowing down and stressing numbers, names and dates.
*These are the words people mishear most.*

❌ Slowing down **everything** to sound clear.
✅ Fast on easy chunks, slow on key points.
*Speaking slowly all the time sounds unnatural and tiring. Contrast is what makes speech clear.*

❌ "Sorry for my English, I speak too fast." (and continuing at the same speed)
✅ "Sorry, I'm talking way too fast." … and actually slowing down.
*The phrase only works if you change gear after it.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

### Part A: Live conversation #2 (40 min)

See [Live conversations](../../toolkit/live-conversations.md).

**Your mission: control the gears.** Have a normal conversation, telling at least one story and explaining at least one thing.

Try these 4 things:
1. Use one brake phrase before an important point ("So here's the thing…").
2. Say every number, name and date slowly and clearly.
3. Tell one story with a fast background ("So-I-was-at-work-right…") and a slow key moment.
4. If you notice you're rushing, say "Sorry, I'm talking way too fast," and change gear.

Bonus: ask your partner, "Was my speed OK? Too fast, too slow?" Write down the answer.

**Backup plan:** if your partner cancels, open [voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md) and paste:

```
Let's have a 30-minute casual voice chat. You're Taylor, a friend who just got back from a trip. Ask me lots of questions about my week, my work and my plans, and tell me short stories about your trip too. I'm practising speed control: fast on easy parts, slow on important points, numbers and names. Twice during the chat, ask me to give you some details with numbers (times, prices, dates, addresses). At the end, tell me if any numbers or names came through wrong, and quote one sentence where I seemed to rush.
```

### Part B: Speed ladder (30 min)

This is a new version of the 3/2/1 drill ([how 3/2/1 works](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md)). Instead of making the story shorter each time, you keep it the same length and change the **speed control**.

**Prompt:** "Tell me about the best or worst trip you've ever taken: where you went, who with, what went right or wrong, and what you'd do differently." (Recycles [Week 5](../../month-02-speed/week-05-better-stories/README.md) storytelling.)

1. **Round 1 (2 min): slow and clear.** Tell it at about 110 WPM, with lots of pauses. Every word clear.
2. **Round 2 (2 min): fast.** Tell it at your fastest comfortable speed, around 170+. Keep the sounds clear.
3. **Round 3 (2 min): controlled.** Fast through the background, slow at the key moment, brake phrases before important points, a power pause before the ending.
4. **Listen (10 min):** listen to all three. Which one would a native speaker enjoy most? (It's usually Round 3.)
5. **Repeat Round 3 (2 min)** with a new prompt: "Explain the most important thing you've learned about your job."
6. **Measure (remaining time):** count the words in 1 minute of Round 3. Write your WPM in the [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).

## 4. Review (15 min)

1. **Spaced review out loud (8 min):** yesterday's sound phrases ("I think so", "Are you sure?"…), last week's (Week 21) and one page from last month (Week 17). Say each phrase twice: once fast (like a chunk), once slow and clear. See [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md).
2. **Stubborn ten check (2 min):** which phrases still have fewer than 2 ticks? Plan to use them in tomorrow's live conversation.
3. **Log tonight (5 min):** 5 phrases, for example "So here's the thing…", "Let me put it this way…", "Just to be clear…", "Sorry, I'm talking way too fast", "Bear with me." Write your WPM (easy topic and hard topic) and your partner's comment in your [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md).
