## Before you start

Tonight's angle: the **other** person is upset, and the emotion is rising. A neighbour is angry about noise, a teammate feels you took credit, a friend is hurt you forgot something. In those moments, the problem isn't vocabulary: it's that stress makes you fast, defensive and flat.
- You'll use your speed control from [Week 22 Day 5](../week-22-sounding-natural/day-5-controlling-your-speed.md) and validation from [Week 21 Day 3](../week-21-deep-conversations/day-3-giving-advice-and-support.md).

## Learning objectives

- Use a calm, low, slow voice when someone is upset (and know why it works).
- Follow the **de-escalation ladder**: acknowledge → slow down → explain → solve.
- Take responsibility for your part without over-apologising or blaming.
- Separate a misunderstanding from a real disagreement ("I think we're talking about two different things").

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's feature: a calm, low, steady voice.** When a good moderator or mediator calms a heated discussion, their voice goes **lower and slower**, not louder. Short sentences. Falling tone. No rushing. That calm voice is contagious: the other person often starts to copy it.

Start with your 5-minute sound routine. Then say each line 3 times, lower and slower each time. ↘ = falling tone.

1. "OK. I can tell you're REALLY frustrated. ↘"
2. "Let's take a STEP back for a second. ↘"
3. "I hear what you're SAYing. ↘"
4. "Can I explain where I'm coming FROM? ↘"
5. "That's NOT what I meant. Let me try AGAIN. ↘"
6. "You're RIGHT. I should've told you EARlier. ↘"
7. "I'm not trying to BLAME anyone. ↘"
8. "What would a GOOD outcome look like for you?"
9. "Let's figure this out toGETHer. ↘"
10. "I need a MINute to think about that. ↘"

**Find a clip:** a moderated debate, a calm podcast host handling an angry caller, or a customer-service training video. Search for "debate moderator calms argument", "de-escalation conversation example", "calm customer service angry customer". Speak along only with the **calm** person. See [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Handling conflict calmly

### Explanation

When someone is angry with you, your body reacts: your heart beats faster, your voice goes up, and you start talking fast to defend yourself. In a second language, this is when your English falls apart. The goal tonight is to have **automatic calm phrases**, so you don't need to think.

**The de-escalation ladder** (go up one step at a time):

| Step | What you do | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Acknowledge** | Show you see their feeling. Don't argue yet. | "I can tell you're really frustrated." |
| **2. Slow down** | Lower your voice, slow down, maybe suggest a pause. | "OK. Let's take a step back for a second." |
| **3. Explain** | Give your side, briefly, without blaming. | "Can I explain where I'm coming from?" |
| **4. Solve** | Move to the future: what happens next? | "What would a good outcome look like for you?" |

Most people skip step 1 and go straight to step 3 ("But I didn't do anything!"). That makes the other person angrier, because they feel unheard.

**Three more tools:**

- **Own your part.** If you made a mistake, say it simply and clearly, once: "You're right. I should've told you earlier." Then move on. Don't over-apologise for 2 minutes, and don't apologise for things that aren't your fault.
- **Check for a misunderstanding.** Many conflicts are just confusion: "Hold on, I think we're talking about two different things. You mean the Monday deadline, and I mean the final one?"
- **Use "I" statements, not "you" statements.** "You never tell me anything!" → "I felt left out when I found out from someone else."

**Your voice matters as much as your words.** A calm phrase said fast and high sounds anxious. Said low and slow, it sounds confident. Use your brake phrases from [Week 22 Day 5](../week-22-sounding-natural/day-5-controlling-your-speed.md).

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| I can tell you're really frustrated. | acknowledging their feeling (step 1) | "I can tell you're really frustrated about this." |
| I hear what you're saying. | showing you've listened | "I hear what you're saying, and it's fair." |
| Let's take a step back for a second. | slowing a heated moment down (step 2) | "OK, let's take a step back for a second." |
| Can I explain where I'm coming from? | asking to give your side (step 3) | "Can I explain where I'm coming from? Then you can tell me if I'm wrong." |
| I think we might be talking about two different things. | spotting a misunderstanding | "Wait, I think we might be talking about two different things." |
| That's not what I meant. Let me try again. | fixing something you said badly | "Sorry, that's not what I meant. Let me try again." |
| You're right. I should've… | owning your mistake | "You're right. I should've checked with you first." |
| I'm not trying to blame anyone. | lowering defensiveness | "I'm not trying to blame anyone, I just wanna fix it." |
| I felt… when… | an "I" statement instead of an accusation | "I felt kind of left out when I wasn't in the meeting." |
| What would a good outcome look like for you? | moving to solutions (step 4) | "OK, so what would a good outcome look like for you?" |
| Let's figure this out together. | showing you're on the same side | "We both want this to work. Let's figure it out together." |
| Can we come back to this tomorrow? | when it's too heated to solve now | "I wanna get this right. Can we come back to this tomorrow?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: The noisy neighbor**

> Neighbor: Your music was SO loud last night. Again! I have to work at six!
> You: Oh no. I can tell you're really frustrated, and that's fair. *(acknowledge)*
> Neighbor: It's the third time this month!
> You: OK. You're right, I should've kept it down. I'm sorry. *(own your part, once)* Can I ask what time it's a problem for you? *(solve)*
> Neighbor: Anything after ten, honestly.
> You: OK, that's totally reasonable. After ten, I'll use headphones. And if it ever happens again, just knock on my door, OK?
> Neighbor: …OK. Thanks. I appreciate that.

**Example 2: The teammate and the credit**

> Teammate: So you presented MY research to the director like it was yours?
> You: Whoa, OK. I can tell you're upset. Let's take a step back for a second. *(acknowledge, slow down)*
> Teammate: I just don't think that's fair.
> You: I hear you. Can I explain where I'm coming from? I mentioned your name on the first slide, but I think I went too fast and nobody noticed. That's on me. *(explain, own)*
> Teammate: I didn't hear it.
> You: That makes sense. What would a good outcome look like for you? I could send the director a message today saying the research was yours. *(solve)*
> Teammate: Yeah. That would help.

**Example 3: A misunderstanding**

> Friend: I can't believe you're not coming on Saturday.
> You: Wait, hold on. I think we might be talking about two different things. I said I can't come to the dinner on Friday. Saturday's the birthday, right? I'm totally coming to that.
> Friend: Oh! Oh my god. OK. Never mind!

### Common mistakes

❌ "Calm down!"
✅ "I can tell you're really frustrated."
*Telling someone to calm down almost always makes them angrier.*

❌ "But it's not my fault! You didn't tell me!"
✅ "I hear what you're saying. Can I explain where I'm coming from?"
*Defending first makes people feel unheard. Acknowledge first.*

❌ "You always do this."
✅ "I felt frustrated when this happened again."
*"Always" and "never" are attacks. "I" statements describe your experience.*

❌ "I'm so sorry, I'm so sorry, I'm terrible, it's all my fault, sorry…"
✅ "You're right. I should've told you. I'm sorry." (once, then solve)
*Over-apologising sounds anxious and makes it about you, not the solution.*

❌ Talking faster and louder as they get angrier.
✅ Getting slower and lower as they get angrier.
*Your calm voice slows them down too.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min): Voice mode with Claude

See [Voice mode with Claude](../../toolkit/voice-mode-with-claude.md). Record on your phone too.

**Step 1 (2 min):** Open voice mode and paste this prompt:

```
Tonight we're doing conflict role-plays so I can practise staying calm in English. You'll play three characters, one at a time, about 15 minutes each. I'll say "next one" to move on. Each character starts upset and gets MORE upset if I'm defensive, fast or dismissive, and calms down if I acknowledge their feelings, slow down, own my part and suggest a solution. Be realistic but not abusive.

1. Pat, my neighbor, who is angry because my delivery packages keep blocking the shared hallway.
2. Morgan, a teammate, who feels I ignored their idea in a meeting and then suggested something similar myself.
3. Jordan, a close friend, who is hurt because I forgot their birthday dinner.

Use casual American English. Don't correct me during the role-plays. After each one, step out of character and tell me: which de-escalation step I did well, one moment I sounded defensive, and one more natural phrase I could have used.
```

**Step 2 (45 min): the three role-plays.** Focus on:
- **Your voice:** lower and slower as they get angrier. Use brake phrases.
- **The ladder:** acknowledge → slow down → explain → solve. Don't skip step 1.
- **Owning your part** once, clearly, without over-apologising.
- At least 3 of your stubborn ten.

**Step 3 (13 min): the hard replay.** Say: "Let's replay character 2, but make Morgan much angrier this time, and interrupt me." Stay calm. If they interrupt you, use "Can I just finish my point?" ([Week 13 Day 3](../../month-04-real-life-pressure/week-13-group-conversations/day-3-holding-your-turn.md)) in a calm voice.

**Step 4 (10 min): feedback.** Ask:

```
Thanks. Out of character now: give me (1) the 5 most useful corrections from tonight, with my sentence and the natural version, (2) the moment where I handled the conflict best, and (3) one phrase I should make automatic for these situations.
```

## 4. Review (15 min)

1. **Log Claude's corrections (5 min)** in your [phrase log](../../toolkit/phrase-log.md). Say each correct version 3 times, in a calm, low voice.
2. **Spaced review out loud (7 min):** yesterday's negotiation phrases, last week's (Week 22) and one page from last month (Week 20: difficult conversations).
3. **Stubborn ten check and [nightly log](../../toolkit/nightly-log.md) (3 min).**
