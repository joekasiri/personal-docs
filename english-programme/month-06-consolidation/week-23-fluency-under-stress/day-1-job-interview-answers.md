## Learning objectives

- Use a casual **STAR** shape (Situation, Task, Action, Result) plus a lesson, said naturally, not like a robot.
- Answer the 5 most common interview questions in under 2 minutes each.
- Talk about strengths and weaknesses honestly, without sounding arrogant or negative.
- Recover smoothly when you don't have a perfect example.

## 1. Shadowing (15 min)

**Tonight's feature: confident openings at full speed.** Strong candidates start answers quickly and calmly, with a short opener that tells the listener where they're going: "Yeah, so a good example of that is…", "Sure. So, at my last company…". The opener is fast and relaxed. Then they slow down slightly for the key point (your [Week 22 Day 5](../week-22-sounding-natural/day-5-controlling-your-speed.md) gear change).

Start with your 5-minute sound routine from Week 22. Then say each line 3 times, the last time at full speed.

1. "Yeah, so a good example of that is… a project I led LAST year."
2. "Sure. So basically, I'm a product designer with about six YEARS of experience."
3. "That's a great question. Let me think of the BEST example."
4. "The situation was… we had two WEEKS to redesign the whole checkout."
5. "What I ended up doing was… talking to FIVE users in two days."
6. "And the result was… conversions went up by about twelve PERcent."
7. "What I took away from that is… always test EARly."
8. "Honestly, something I'm still working on is… delegating."
9. "What drew me to this role is… the focus on ACcessibility."
10. "I'd say my biggest strength is… making complicated things SIMple."

**Find a clip:** a real or mock job interview with a confident candidate. Search for "mock interview product manager", "behavioral interview example answers", "designer job interview real". Use **speak-along** at full speed: talk exactly with the candidate's answers, matching the fast openers. See [Shadowing](../../toolkit/shadowing.md).

## 2. Main skill (20 min): Job interview answers

### Explanation

Interview questions come in two types:

1. **"Tell me about…" questions** (Tell me about yourself. Why this job? What are your strengths?): you can partly prepare these, and you did in Week 8.
2. **Behavioral questions** ("Tell me about a time when…"): the interviewer wants a **real story**. These are where nervous candidates either freeze or ramble.

For behavioral questions, use the **STAR** shape, but say it like a person, not a template:

| Part | What it is | Natural phrase |
|---|---|---|
| **S**ituation | Where, when, the background (1–2 sentences) | "So, at my last job, we were…" |
| **T**ask | What *you* had to do (1 sentence) | "My job was to…" / "I was responsible for…" |
| **A**ction | What *you* did (the longest part, 3–5 sentences) | "What I ended up doing was…", "First I…, then I…" |
| **R**esult | What happened, with a number if possible | "The result was…", "In the end…" |
| **+ Lesson** | What you learned (the part most people forget) | "What I took away from that is…" |

Three things make answers sound fluent and confident:

1. **Say "I", not "we", for the action.** Interviewers want to know what *you* did. "We redesigned it" → "I ran the user interviews and led the redesign."
2. **Keep it under 2 minutes.** Long answers sound like you're lost. If they want more, they'll ask. End clearly: "…so yeah, that's what I learned from it."
3. **It's OK to take 2 seconds.** "That's a great question, let me think of the best example" is completely normal, and much better than "umm". You know this from [Week 20](../../month-05-work-and-complex-topics/week-20-thinking-on-your-feet/README.md).

**Strengths and weaknesses:**
- **Strength** = a skill + a short proof. "I'd say my biggest strength is making complicated things simple. For example, last year I…"
- **Weakness** = a real one + what you're doing about it. "Something I'm working on is speaking up in big meetings. I've been practising a lot, and I now present our team's updates every week." (Notice: this one is true for you, and it's a great answer.)

### Key phrases

| Phrase | Use it when… | Example |
|---|---|---|
| A good example of that is… | starting a behavioral answer | "A good example of that is a redesign I led last spring." |
| The situation was… | setting up the story | "The situation was, our app had a really high drop-off rate." |
| I was responsible for… | stating your task | "I was responsible for the whole onboarding flow." |
| What I ended up doing was… | describing your action | "What I ended up doing was running five quick user tests." |
| The result was… | giving the outcome | "The result was a 20% drop in support tickets." |
| What I took away from that is… | ending with a lesson | "What I took away from that is to involve engineers early." |
| I'd say my biggest strength is… | answering the strengths question | "I'd say my biggest strength is staying calm when things go wrong." |
| Something I'm working on is… | answering the weakness question honestly | "Something I'm working on is saying no to extra projects." |
| What drew me to this role is… | answering "Why this job?" | "What drew me to this role is the chance to work on health products." |
| That's a great question, let me think of the best example. | buying 2 seconds | "Hmm, that's a great question. Let me think of the best example." |
| I don't have an exact example of that, but the closest is… | when you don't have a perfect story | "I haven't managed a team yet, but the closest is mentoring two juniors." |
| Does that answer your question? | checking at the end (sparingly) | "…so that's how we fixed it. Does that answer your question?" |

### Examples

**Example 1: A full STAR answer (about 90 seconds)**

> **Interviewer:** Tell me about a time you disagreed with a stakeholder.
> **You:** Sure. A good example of that is a project from last year. The situation was, our head of sales wanted to add a big pop-up to the homepage to push a new plan. I was responsible for the homepage design, and I was pretty sure the pop-up would annoy users. So what I ended up doing was, instead of just saying no, I suggested we test it. We ran an A/B test for two weeks: half the users got the pop-up, half got a small banner. The result was, the banner actually got more sign-ups, and fewer people left the page. So we went with the banner, and the head of sales was happy because the numbers were better. What I took away from that is, data makes disagreements way less personal.

**Example 2: The weakness question**

> **Interviewer:** What's your biggest weakness?
> **You:** Honestly, something I'm working on is speaking up in big meetings, especially in English. In small groups I'm totally fine, but with twenty people, I used to stay quiet even when I had a good idea. So for the last six months, I've been practising every day, and I started volunteering to give our team's weekly update. It's gotten way easier. I'm not perfect yet, but I'm a lot more confident.

**Example 3: No perfect example**

> **Interviewer:** Tell me about a time you managed a team.
> **You:** I haven't officially managed a team yet, but the closest is last year, when I mentored two junior designers for six months. I set up weekly one-on-ones, gave them feedback on their work, and helped one of them present at a company all-hands. It's actually one of the things that drew me to this role: I'd love to do more of that.

### Common mistakes

❌ "We did the research, we designed it, we launched it."
✅ "I ran the research, I designed the main flow, and we launched it together."
*Say "I" for your actions. Interviewers are hiring you, not your team.*

❌ "My weakness is that I'm a perfectionist." (and nothing else)
✅ "Something I'm working on is… and here's what I'm doing about it."
*Fake weaknesses sound rehearsed. A real one plus an action sounds honest and confident.*

❌ A five-minute answer with every detail.
✅ 60–120 seconds, then stop: "…so that's what I learned."
*Long answers lose the listener. Give them space to ask more.*

❌ "Umm… umm… I don't know… maybe…"
✅ "That's a great question. Let me think of the best example." (pause) "OK, so…"
*Two seconds of calm thinking looks good. Panic looks bad.*

❌ "I'm the best designer in my company."
✅ "I'd say my biggest strength is… For example…"
*Proof is more convincing than big claims.*

## 3. Speaking drill (70 min)

Mixed drills tonight. Record everything. Hardest first: the panel role-play.

**Part A: Panel role-play (25 min)**
Imagine a panel of three interviewers for a product design role (or your own job). Each has a style:
- **Dana (friendly hiring manager):** asks "Tell me about yourself" and "Why this role?"
- **Raj (tough engineering lead):** asks short, direct follow-ups: "Why?", "What would you do differently?", "What was *your* part?"
- **Lee (HR):** asks about strengths, weaknesses and conflict.

Use the questions in [Exercise 1.1](exercises.md#day-1-exercises). Read a question, answer it straight away (no preparation), then read one of Raj's follow-ups and answer that too. Do 6 questions in total. Stand up while you answer. It makes it feel more real and helps your voice.

**Part B: 3/2/1 "Tell me about yourself" (20 min)** ([how to do the drill](../../toolkit/drill-3-2-1.md))
**Prompt:** "Tell me about yourself", as you'd answer it at the start of an interview for your dream job. Include: who you are professionally, one achievement, what you're looking for next and why this kind of role.
3 minutes, 2 minutes, then 1 minute. Compare the 1-minute version with your Week 8 version (if you have it). Notice how much more natural it is now.

**Part C: Rapid-fire interview round (15 min)** ([random-topic drill](../../toolkit/random-topic-drill.md))
Shuffle questions 11–20 from [Exercise 1.1](exercises.md#day-1-exercises). Answer each one in **exactly 60 seconds** (set a timer). Start talking within 2 seconds of reading the question.

**Part D: Listen back (10 min)**
Listen to your best and worst answers from Part A. For each, check: Did I have all of S-T-A-R + lesson? Did I say "I" for the actions? Was it under 2 minutes? Write down one improvement. Re-answer your worst question once more.
