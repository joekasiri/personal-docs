# Week 23 exercises: Fluency under stress
[Week 23 overview](README.md) · [Day 1](day-1-job-interview-answers.md) · [Day 2](day-2-being-put-on-the-spot.md) · [Day 3](day-3-negotiating-and-persuading.md) · [Day 4](day-4-handling-conflict-calmly.md) · [Day 5](day-5-talking-to-strangers.md) · [Day 6](day-6-weekly-check.md)

**How to use these exercises:** everything is **out loud**, and this week everything should be a little **uncomfortable**: use a timer, stand up, speak a bit louder than normal, and don't stop when you make a mistake. Answer first, then look at the model answers. The model answers are examples for a product designer. Change the details to fit your own life and work.

## Day 1 exercises

### Exercise 1.1: 20 interview questions

**Time:** 10 min tonight (and used in the drill) · **Type:** out loud, record

1. Tonight in the main skill: read the **model answers for questions 1–5** in the answer key out loud, once, with natural speed and stress.
2. Then answer **three** of the behavioral questions (6–15) in your own words, using S-T-A-R + lesson. Under 2 minutes each.
3. In the drill, you'll use the rest of the list, plus Raj's follow-ups.

**Core questions**
1. Tell me about yourself.
2. Why do you want this job?
3. What's your biggest strength?
4. What's your biggest weakness?
5. Where do you see yourself in five years?

**Behavioral questions ("Tell me about a time…")**
6. …you disagreed with a stakeholder or your manager.
7. …you made a mistake at work.
8. …you had to meet a really tight deadline.
9. …you had to explain something complicated to a non-expert.
10. …you got critical feedback.
11. …you worked with a difficult teammate.
12. …you had to make a decision without all the information.
13. …a project failed.
14. …you showed leadership without being the manager.
15. …you used data or research to change someone's mind.

**Tricky questions**

17. What are your salary expectations?
18. What would your current manager say about you?
19. Tell me about something you taught yourself.
20. Do you have any questions for us?

**Raj's follow-ups** (use after any answer in the drill):
- "Why did you choose that approach?"
- "What was *your* part, specifically?"
- "What would you do differently?"
- "How did you measure success?"
- "What did the other person think?"

### Exercise 1.2: STAR skeletons

**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

Each skeleton is a set of notes. Turn it into a **full, natural spoken answer** (60–90 seconds), using the STAR phrases from [Day 1](day-1-job-interview-answers.md). Then do the same with notes from **your own** story.

**Skeleton A: "Tell me about a time you had a tight deadline."**
- S: fintech app, regulator rule change, 10 days to update sign-up flow
- T: me = lead designer for sign-up
- A: cut scope to 3 must-have screens; daily 15-min check-ins with engineers; reused existing components
- R: launched on day 9, zero compliance issues
- Lesson: small scope + daily contact beats long hours

**Skeleton B: "Tell me about a mistake you made."**
- S: e-commerce redesign, I shipped a new filter design
- T: me = owned filters
- A: skipped testing on mobile because of time; complaints came in; I owned it in stand-up, fixed it in 2 days, added a mobile check to our checklist
- R: complaints stopped; the checklist is now used by the whole team
- Lesson: always test on the smallest screen first

**Skeleton C: "Tell me about a time you explained something complicated."**
- S: our sales team didn't understand why the new pricing page was simpler
- T: me = explain the research
- A: replaced a 30-slide deck with 3 user video clips + 1 chart
- R: sales team started using the clips with customers
- Lesson: show, don't tell

**Skeleton D (your own):** write 5 short lines (S, T, A, R, lesson) for your own success story and your own failure story, then say each one out loud.

### Exercise 1.3: Strength and weakness

**Time:** 4 min · **Type:** out loud

**Part 1: Strength + proof.** Choose 3 strengths from this list that are true for you. For each one, say: "I'd say one of my strengths is… For example, …" (30 seconds each).

making complex things simple · staying calm under pressure · working with engineers · user research · attention to detail · giving clear feedback · learning fast · organising messy projects · presenting ideas · bringing people together

**Part 2: Weakness + action.** Say the frame with your own weakness: "Something I'm working on is… What I've been doing about it is… And it's already gotten better: for example…" (30–45 seconds).

### Answer key

**Model answers, Exercise 1.1** (for a product designer; adapt to your life):

1. **Tell me about yourself.** "Sure. So, I'm a product designer with about six years of experience, mostly in mobile apps. Right now I'm at a fintech company, where I lead the design for our onboarding and payments. The thing I'm proudest of is a redesign last year that cut sign-up drop-off by about a third. I really love the research side, like talking to users and turning messy problems into simple flows. And now I'm looking for a role where I can work on bigger, more complex products, and maybe mentor other designers. Which is a big part of why this role caught my eye."
2. **Why this job?** "What drew me to this role is two things, really. First, your product is used by millions of people every day, and the design problems at that scale are super interesting to me. And second, I've read a lot about how your team does research, and that's exactly how I like to work."
3. **Strength.** "I'd say my biggest strength is making complicated things simple. For example, last year our payments flow had eleven steps. I mapped out every step with the engineers, found which ones were only there for historical reasons, and we got it down to four."
4. **Weakness.** "Honestly, something I'm working on is speaking up in big meetings. In small groups I'm totally fine, but with twenty people I used to stay quiet. So for the past six months I've been practising a lot, and I now give our team's weekly update. It's gotten way easier."
5. **Five years.** "Hmm, good question. In five years, I'd love to be leading a small design team, still close to the actual design work, but also helping other designers grow. I'm already mentoring two juniors, so I'm kind of working toward it."
6. **Disagreed with a stakeholder.** "A good example is when our head of sales wanted a big pop-up on the homepage. I was responsible for the homepage, and I thought it'd annoy users. So instead of just saying no, I suggested an A/B test: pop-up versus a small banner. The banner got more sign-ups, so we went with it, and sales was happy because the numbers were better. What I took away from that is that data makes disagreements way less personal."
7. **A mistake.** "Sure. Last year I shipped a new filter design without testing it properly on mobile, because we were short on time. Complaints started coming in the next day. I owned it in stand-up, fixed it in two days, and added a mobile check to our design checklist. The whole team uses that checklist now. Lesson learned: always test on the smallest screen first."
8. **Tight deadline.** "The situation was, a new regulation meant we had ten days to update our whole sign-up flow. I was the lead designer. What I ended up doing was cutting it down to the three screens we really had to change, doing quick daily check-ins with the engineers, and reusing components we already had. We launched on day nine, with no compliance issues. What I took away from that is that small scope and daily contact beat long hours."
9. **Explaining something complicated.** "Our sales team didn't understand why we'd simplified the pricing page. I was asked to explain the research, and instead of a thirty-slide deck, I showed them three short clips of real users getting confused by the old page, plus one chart. Everyone got it in five minutes, and they started using the clips with customers. So, show, don't tell."
10. **Critical feedback.** "My manager once told me my presentations were too detailed: people got lost. Honestly, it stung a bit at first. But I asked her for an example, and she was right. So now I start every presentation with one slide: the problem, the solution, and what I need from them. People actually make decisions in my meetings now."
11. **Difficult teammate.** "I worked with an engineer who'd push back on almost every design. It was frustrating. What I ended up doing was inviting him to the user tests. Once he saw users struggle, he became one of the biggest supporters of the redesign. I learned that people often push back because they're not included early enough."
12. **Decision without all the information.** "We had to decide on a navigation structure before the research was finished, because engineering needed to start. So I made the best guess from the data we had, and I built in a checkpoint: we'd test it with five users in week two. One part didn't work, and we changed it early, when it was still cheap."
13. **A project that failed.** "We built a social feature that we were really excited about, and almost nobody used it. Looking back, we skipped validating whether users actually wanted it. It was a tough lesson, but now I always push for a small test, even a fake button, before we build something big."
14. **Leadership without being the manager.** "Our team didn't have a design system, and everything looked slightly different. Nobody asked me to, but I started a small library of shared components and ran a monthly session to review them. A year later, it's the official system for the whole company."
15. **Data to change someone's mind.** "Our product manager wanted to remove the 'guest checkout' option. I pulled the data and found that about forty percent of first-time buyers used it. When I showed that, plus a couple of user quotes, we kept it, and made it more visible instead."
16. **Why leaving?** "I've learned a lot where I am, and I really like my team. But I've kind of reached the ceiling in terms of the size of the problems I can work on. I'm looking for a bigger challenge, and that's exactly what this role offers."
17. **Salary.** "Based on my research for this kind of role and my experience, I'm looking for something in the range of X to Y. But I'm open, and I'd love to hear more about the whole package."
18. **What would your manager say?** "I think she'd say I'm the person who makes messy projects clear, and that I'm calm when things go wrong. She'd probably also say I could speak up more in big meetings, which is something I've been actively working on."
19. **Something you taught yourself.** "Prototyping in code. I'm not an engineer, but I taught myself enough to build simple interactive prototypes. It took a few months of evenings, but now I can test ideas with users much faster."
20. **Questions for us?** "Yes, a few. What does success look like in this role after six months? How do designers and engineers work together here? And what's the biggest challenge the team is facing right now?"

**Exercise 1.2, model answer for Skeleton A:**
"Sure. So, the situation was, I was working at a fintech company, and a new regulation came in. We had ten days to update our whole sign-up flow. I was the lead designer for sign-up. What I ended up doing was, first, cutting the scope down to the three screens that legally had to change. ### Exercise 2.2: Short version

### Exercise 3.2: Eight scenarios

**Time:** 8 min tonight (read the cards), then 35 min in the drill · **Type:** role-play, both sides

For each scenario, there are two role cards. Play **both** people, out loud. Each person has a goal and a limit (the point where they say "no"). Reach a deal or a clear "let me sleep on it".

**1. The salary**
- **You:** You've been offered a product designer job at $85,000. You want $95,000. Your limit: $90,000, or $87,000 plus one extra week of vacation.
- **Them (hiring manager Dana):** Budget is up to $92,000, but you'd prefer to stay under $90,000. You can offer extra vacation, a training budget or a review after 6 months.

**2. The deadline**
- **You:** Your PM wants the full redesign (20 screens) by Friday. Realistic: 8 key screens by Friday, the rest next Wednesday. You need final copy by Tuesday.
- **Them (PM Chris):** The CEO demo is on Monday. Only the checkout screens really matter for the demo, but you don't say that at first.

**3. The rent increase**
- **You:** Your landlord wants to raise the rent from $1,800 to $2,000. You've been a good tenant for 3 years. Your limit: $1,900. You could sign a 2-year lease.
- **Them (landlord Pat):** You want $2,000 but finding a new tenant takes 2 months and costs money. A 2-year lease would be very attractive.

**4. The freelance rate**
- **You:** A small company wants you to design their website for $3,000. You usually charge $5,000 for that size. You could do it for $4,000 if they accept fewer pages or let you use the project in your portfolio.
- **Them (founder Lee):** Your budget is $3,500 max. You need it done in 4 weeks. You'd happily give portfolio rights and a testimonial.

**5. The used bike**
- **You (buyer):** The bike is listed at $300. You want to pay $220. Your limit: $260. You can pay cash today and pick it up now.
- **Them (seller):** You want at least $240, and you want to sell it today because you're moving.

**6. The vacation plans**
- **You:** Your friend group wants the beach again. You want the mountains. You'd accept the beach if you can choose the house, or a mountain trip next year.
- **Them (friend Jess):** Wants the beach because it's easy and relaxing. Would try the mountains if it's not more expensive and someone else plans it.

**7. The broken product**
- **You (customer):** Your headphones broke after 5 weeks. The store's return policy is 30 days. You want a full refund. Your limit: a replacement.
- **Them (store manager):** Policy is 30 days, but you can offer a replacement or store credit for a good customer. A full refund needs your boss's approval.

**8. The project scope**
- **You (designer):** A client wants to add 3 new features to the project without changing the deadline or price. You can add 1 feature for free, or all 3 with 2 more weeks or $2,000 more.
- **Them (client Morgan):** The deadline can move a week, no more. A small extra budget ($1,000) is possible. The most important new feature is the dashboard.

**Part 2: Gap-fill.** Read the dialogue out loud, saying the missing phrase in each gap.

> Neighbor: Your packages are blocking the hallway AGAIN. I almost fell!
> You: Oh no. (1) ______ you're really frustrated. Are you OK?
> Neighbor: I'm fine, but this is the fourth time!
> You: (2) ______. I should've moved them sooner. I'm sorry.
> Neighbor: You always say that.

### Exercise 4.3: Three conflict scenarios

### Exercise 5.2: The 5-minute map

A 5-minute stranger conversation often follows this map. Practise it out loud with **3 different imaginary strangers** (a woman with a laptop at a co-working space, an older man at a dog park, a student at a meetup). Play both sides.

| Minute | What happens | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **0:00** | Situational opener | "Is that the new co-working plan? Is it worth it?" |
| **0:30** | Answer, add, ask back | "Oh, nice. I just started here. Do you come every day?" |
| **1:00** | Introduce yourself | "I'm Sam, by the way." |
| **1:30** | Find a shared topic (work, the place, the city, an event) | "So what do you do?" → "No way, I'm in design too." |
| **2:30** | Go one rung deeper ([Week 21 Day 1](../week-21-deep-conversations/day-1-from-small-talk-to-real-talk.md)) | "How'd you end up doing that?" |
| **4:00** | A light comment or a laugh | "Ha, I think every designer has that story." |
| **4:30** | Warm exit, maybe swap contacts | "It was really nice chatting. Are you here on Thursdays too?" |

### Exercise 5.3: Accent questions and exits

**Time:** 6 min · **Type:** out loud

**Part 1: The accent question.** Answer each version confidently, in 2–3 sentences, and end with a question back. No apologies!

1. "Where's your accent from?"
2. "You're not from here, are you?"
3. "Your English is really good! Where did you learn it?"
4. "Is that a French accent?" (It isn't.)

**Part 2: Exits.** Say a warm exit for each situation.

1. Your coffee is ready.
2. The talk at the meetup is about to start.
3. The person keeps looking at their phone.
4. You had a great chat and want to stay in touch.
5. You need to go find your friend at the party.
6. Your train stop is next.
7. The conversation has become awkward.
8. You want to join a different group at the event.

## Day 6 materials

### Stress missions

Choose **one**. Pick the one that scares you a little, not the one that scares you a lot.

1. **Go first.** At a meetup or group event, volunteer to introduce yourself or share an idea **first**.
2. **The recommendation ask.** In a shop, restaurant or bar, ask a staff member for a personal recommendation and two follow-up questions ("What's your favorite? Why? What would you pair it with?").
3. **The friendly negotiation.** At a market, a garage sale or anywhere where it's normal, ask politely for a better price ("Is there any flexibility if I take two?").
4. **The group join.** At an event, walk up to a group of 2–3 people already talking and join with "Sorry, I couldn't help overhearing…" or "Mind if I join you?".
5. **The mock interview.** Ask a colleague, friend or tutor to interview you for 15 minutes with real questions, including follow-ups. Then ask for feedback.
6. **Speak up at work.** In a real meeting, disagree politely with one point, or volunteer to summarise the next steps ([Week 17 Day 5](../../month-05-work-and-complex-topics/week-17-meetings/day-5-summing-up-next-steps.md)).
7. **The long stranger chat.** Have one conversation with a stranger that lasts 10 minutes or more.
